# FWSD-003 — Early Buddhist Teaching Corpus Foundational Witness Selection Dossier

**Version:** 0.1  
**Status:** CAND-3 — Evaluated; not canonically admitted  
**Parent protocol:** EPC-004 — Foundational Witness Selection Protocol  
**Candidate:** FWC-CAND-006 — Early Buddhist Teaching Corpus  
**Prepared:** 2026-09-21  
**Scope:** Historical-critical evaluation of the earliest recoverable Buddhist teaching associated with Gautama Buddha, using cross-school textual comparison rather than treating any one later canon as a verbatim transcript

---

## 1. Executive assessment

The Early Buddhist Teaching Corpus (EBTC) is one of the strongest candidates presently identified for eventual foundational reception because a substantial doctrinal and practical core can be recovered across early textual traditions that later belonged to different Buddhist schools.

The candidate is **not**:

- the entire Pāli Canon;
- the Theravāda tradition as a whole;
- every discourse attributed to the Buddha;
- later Abhidhamma/Abhidharma systematics;
- Mahāyāna sūtras;
- Madhyamaka emptiness doctrine;
- Yogācāra;
- Buddha-nature doctrine;
- tantric/Vajrayāna materials;
- Buddhaghosa's commentarial synthesis;
- or the complete traditional biography of Siddhārtha Gautama.

The historically responsible candidate is the **shared early teaching complex** recoverable through comparison of:

- Pāli Nikāyas;
- Chinese Āgamas;
- surviving Sanskrit and Prakrit/Gāndhārī parallels;
- early Vinaya traditions;
- and, where useful, the earliest manuscript witnesses.

The strongest recoverable elements include:

- a historical renunciant teacher Gautama standing behind the Buddhist movement;
- diagnosis of dukkha / unsatisfactoriness / suffering;
- causal analysis centered especially on craving and ignorance;
- the possibility of cessation/liberation;
- a disciplined path combining ethical conduct, mental cultivation, and insight;
- a "middle way" rejecting both sensual indulgence and destructive asceticism;
- dependent arising / conditionality;
- impermanence;
- non-self analysis of the aggregates;
- morally consequential intention/action;
- meditation and concentration;
- mindfulness/attentive observation;
- renunciation and reduction of craving;
- non-harming and ethical restraint;
- and the claim that awakening is a transformation achievable through disciplined practice rather than through sacrificial status or creator-deity mediation.

The principal limitations are equally important:

- no text survives from Gautama's lifetime;
- his exact dates remain debated;
- the earliest large textual corpora were orally transmitted for centuries;
- school-specific recensions differ;
- the earliest surviving manuscripts postdate the founder by centuries;
- complete discourses can contain creative oral composition and later framing;
- the traditional life story contains substantial hagiographic development;
- and several central doctrines admit materially different philosophical interpretations.

**Procedural conclusion:** retain the EBTC at **CAND-3 — Evaluated**. It should advance to full cross-candidate comparison and is a serious future **CAND-4 — Foundational Candidate**, but no FWC-A admission should occur before the first comparative review cycle is complete.

## 2. Candidate identity and source freeze

### 2.1 Included witness

FWSD-003 evaluates the **earliest recoverable Buddhist teaching complex associated with Gautama Buddha**.

Its unit of analysis is not a later canonical book as such, but a teaching, formula, practice, discourse core, or institutional pattern that can be compared across early recensions.

### 2.2 Primary source families

The principal source families are:

1. **Pāli Nikāyas**
   - Dīgha Nikāya;
   - Majjhima Nikāya;
   - Saṃyutta Nikāya;
   - Aṅguttara Nikāya;
   - selected early portions of the Khuddaka corpus where independently justified.

2. **Chinese Āgamas**
   - Dīrgha Āgama;
   - Madhyama Āgama;
   - Saṃyukta Āgama;
   - Ekottarika Āgama;
   - preserved from non-Theravāda school lineages.

3. **Early Vinaya traditions**
   - used especially where parallel disciplinary or institutional material can be compared across schools.

4. **Sanskrit, Gāndhārī, and other early fragments**
   - especially where they provide independent recensional witnesses or early manuscript evidence.

5. **Early inscriptions and archaeology**
   - primarily as controls for the existence and development of Buddhist communities rather than as direct transcripts of Gautama's teaching.

### 2.3 Excluded from automatic founder attribution

The following are not source-frozen into FWSD-003:

- later Abhidhamma/Abhidharma scholastic systems;
- Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka;
- Yogācāra;
- tathāgatagarbha / Buddha-nature doctrine;
- the Mahāyāna bodhisattva system as a whole;
- tantric doctrine or practice;
- Pure Land systems;
- Zen/Chan formulations;
- Buddhaghosa's Visuddhimagga;
- later Jātaka biographies;
- the fully developed cosmic Buddha biography;
- later relic legends;
- or modern secular mindfulness interpretations.

These may contain profound Buddhist insight but require independent provenance.

## 3. Historical Gautama

### 3.1 Historicity

A historical teacher Gautama/Gotama standing behind the Buddhist movement is accepted by the broad center of contemporary scholarship, although a minority of scholars have pressed more skeptical models.

The responsible formulation is:

> **Gautama was probably a historical renunciant teacher active in northern India whose movement became the Buddhist Saṅgha; detailed biography is much less secure than his existence and broad role.**

### 3.2 Date

Current historical scholarship often places Gautama in the **fifth century BCE**, with death probably before 400 BCE, while acknowledging that reliable chronological evidence is scarce.

Traditional chronologies differ materially.

No exact birth or death year shall be treated as historically established by this dossier.

### 3.3 Geography and social world

The early movement belongs to the eastern/northeastern Gangetic world of the mid-first millennium BCE, amid:

- emerging states and republic-like polities;
- urbanization;
- Brahmanical traditions;
- śramaṇa renunciant movements;
- Jain and other ascetic communities;
- and intense debate about karma, rebirth, liberation, sacrifice, asceticism, and selfhood.

**Historical existence:** **High**  
**Exact life chronology:** **Low–Moderate**  
**Broad fifth-century/northern Indian placement:** **Moderate–High**

---

## 4. Biography versus recoverable history

The traditional Buddha biography is a highly developed religious narrative.

Elements such as:

- miraculous conception;
- cosmic birth signs;
- the Four Sights in fixed narrative form;
- Māra's armies;
- supernatural events at awakening;
- and elaborate previous-life stories

must not be assumed to be contemporary historical records.

The earliest canons preserve **biographical fragments**, not one original complete biography.

The historical core most responsibly retained includes:

- a renunciant teacher;
- a claim to awakening;
- teaching activity;
- formation of a community of disciples;
- conflict and interaction with rival renunciants and Brahmins;
- and eventual death remembered by the movement.

The precise narrative architecture of the later Buddha-life remains a separate reception-history object.

---

## 5. Oral transmission

### 5.1 Long oral period

The early Buddhist teaching was transmitted orally for centuries.

Modern reconstruction commonly works with approximately **four centuries of oral transmission** between Gautama's death and the first major written redactions in the last centuries BCE.

### 5.2 Mnemonic preservation

The tradition employed:

- communal recitation;
- repetition;
- fixed formulas;
- numbered lists;
- stock phrases;
- metrical passages;
- and institutional memorization.

These features could preserve teaching structures with considerable stability.

### 5.3 Sources of change

The same oral system also permitted:

- reordering;
- expansion;
- abbreviation;
- formula substitution;
- explanatory interpolation;
- harmonization;
- school-specific adaptation;
- and construction of complete discourses from inherited formulaic material.

Therefore:

> **oral transmission is neither evidence of corruption by itself nor evidence of perfect verbatim preservation.**

---

## 6. Comparative textual method

The strongest method available is **cross-recensional comparison**.

Where a Pāli discourse has a close Chinese Āgama, Sanskrit, or Gāndhārī parallel preserved through a different school lineage, shared structure may predate the sectarian separation of those schools.

Agreement does not prove Gautama's exact wording.

It increases confidence that the material belongs to an early common Buddhist stratum.

Differences between recensions are also evidence.

They help identify:

- later additions;
- local rearrangements;
- school-specific doctrinal emphasis;
- and unstable narrative framing.

This dossier therefore values **shared teaching architecture** more highly than a claim that one existing recension perfectly reproduces the founder's sentences.

---

## 7. Manuscript evidence

The oldest surviving substantial Buddhist manuscripts are Gāndhārī birch-bark texts from roughly the **first century BCE through the third century CE**.

They are much later than Gautama but exceptionally important because they show:

- early textual plurality;
- multiple genres;
- regional Buddhist literatures;
- parallels to texts known from later canons;
- and the existence of written Buddhist transmission long before most surviving South Asian manuscripts.

The manuscript evidence therefore confirms early Buddhist textual diversity rather than one single pristine surviving autograph tradition.

## 8. Recoverability framework

FWSD-003 uses the following bands for teaching units:

**A — Very strong early shared core**  
**B — Probably early / strongly recoverable**  
**C — Plausible early teaching**  
**D — Uncertain or materially school-shaped**  
**E — Better treated as later development for historical-Buddha reconstruction**

The bands describe historical recoverability, not metaphysical truth.

## 9. EBTC core teaching register

### EBTC-001 — Dukkha as a fundamental problem of conditioned existence

**Recoverability:** A

The early Buddhist project begins from the pervasive instability and unsatisfactoriness associated with:

- birth;
- aging;
- illness;
- death;
- loss;
- frustration;
- and clinging to changing psychophysical processes.

"Dukkha" shall not be flattened into the English word **pain** alone.

Its semantic range includes suffering, stress, unsatisfactoriness, and the inability of conditioned phenomena to provide secure lasting satisfaction.

---

### EBTC-002 — Craving is a major causal condition of dukkha

**Recoverability:** A

Early sources repeatedly connect suffering with:

- craving;
- grasping;
- attachment;
- and the drive toward continued becoming.

This does not imply that every form of desire is treated identically.

The historically secure point is the central causal role of craving/attachment in the production and perpetuation of dukkha.

---

### EBTC-003 — Cessation/liberation is possible

**Recoverability:** A

The early corpus does not merely diagnose suffering.

It claims that the causal processes sustaining suffering can cease.

This cessation is associated with:

- release;
- dispassion;
- relinquishment;
- awakening;
- and nibbāna/nirvāṇa.

The metaphysical nature of nibbāna remains separately disputed.

---

### EBTC-004 — A disciplined path leads toward cessation

**Recoverability:** A

The early tradition strongly preserves a practical path integrating:

- right understanding/view;
- intention;
- speech;
- action;
- livelihood;
- effort;
- mindfulness;
- and concentration.

Exact list terminology and pedagogical arrangement may be systematized, but a structured path combining ethics, meditation, and insight is highly secure.

---

### EBTC-005 — The Middle Way rejects both indulgence and destructive self-mortification

**Recoverability:** A/B

The early movement rejects the idea that liberation is obtained merely through:

- sensual indulgence;
- or extreme bodily punishment.

The Middle Way is therefore not originally a generic slogan meaning "moderation in everything."

It is a soteriological rejection of two ineffective extremes and an endorsement of a disciplined path.

---

### EBTC-006 — Conditionality / dependent arising is central

**Recoverability:** A/B

Early Buddhist texts repeatedly teach that phenomena arise and cease according to conditions.

The canonical twelve-link sequence is historically important, but precise formulations may have undergone systematization.

The strongest source-frozen proposition is:

> **suffering and the processes constituting lived existence arise conditionally rather than from an unconditioned permanent personal essence.**

Dependent arising should not be automatically translated into the modern ecological slogan that "everything is connected to everything."

Its early function is strongly tied to the arising and cessation of dukkha and saṃsāric existence.

---

### EBTC-007 — Conditioned phenomena are impermanent

**Recoverability:** A

Impermanence is one of the most widely distributed early Buddhist analytical principles.

Physical and mental processes arise, change, and cease.

Attachment to what is unstable creates suffering.

---

### EBTC-008 — The five aggregates are not-self

**Recoverability:** A/B

Early sources analyze:

- form;
- feeling;
- perception;
- volitional formations;
- and consciousness

as unsuitable for identification as a permanent controlling self.

The famous non-self discourse argues, among other things, from:

- impermanence;
- vulnerability to affliction;
- and lack of complete control.

**Important qualification:** the precise philosophical thesis of **anattā/anātman** remains debated.

The early texts strongly reject identifying the aggregates as an enduring sovereign self.

Whether the founder intended an unrestricted metaphysical proposition:

> "no self of any possible kind exists"

requires more caution than popular summaries often imply.

---

### EBTC-009 — Insight requires de-identification from impermanent processes

**Recoverability:** A/B

Early practice repeatedly asks practitioners to observe bodily and mental phenomena without taking them as:

- "mine";
- "I";
- or "my self."

This has major implications for:

- attachment;
- ego;
- fear of loss;
- and liberation.

It should not automatically be translated into either modern nihilism or Advaita-style hidden Self doctrine.

---

### EBTC-010 — Karma is fundamentally linked to intentional action

**Recoverability:** A/B

The early Buddhist moral world treats intentional action as consequential.

Karma is not merely cosmic fate.

It is closely tied to volition, habit, and morally conditioned consequence.

The texts also embed karma within rebirth.

The historical presence of this teaching is strong.

Its metaphysical truth across lifetimes remains unresolved under EPC-001.

---

### EBTC-011 — Rebirth belongs to the early Buddhist worldview

**Recoverability:** A/B as early teaching  
**Metaphysical status:** unresolved

Repeated birth and death are integral to the early liberation project.

Nibbāna is commonly described as release from the cycle.

A modern reconstruction that strips rebirth entirely from early Buddhism may be philosophically useful but is historically incomplete.

This does not require the Way to affirm rebirth as established fact.

---

### EBTC-012 — Ethical restraint is constitutive of the path

**Recoverability:** A

The early path is not meditation detached from ethics.

Strongly recurrent ethical concerns include:

- non-killing/non-harming;
- refraining from theft;
- sexual restraint;
- truthfulness/right speech;
- restraint concerning intoxicants in lay formulations;
- right livelihood;
- and mental cultivation away from greed, hatred, and delusion.

Specific monastic rules vary and developed over time.

The ethical-practice complex itself is early.

---

### EBTC-013 — Mental intention and motivation matter morally

**Recoverability:** A/B

Early Buddhist ethics is deeply concerned not only with external action but with:

- intention;
- greed;
- hatred;
- delusion;
- generosity;
- goodwill;
- compassion;
- and wisdom.

This makes the system a moral psychology as well as a rule system.

---

### EBTC-014 — Meditation is central to liberation

**Recoverability:** A

The early corpus integrates contemplative training into the path.

Relevant practices include:

- concentration;
- jhāna/dhyāna;
- mindfulness;
- attention to body, feeling, mind, and phenomena;
- cultivation of wholesome mental states;
- and insight into impermanence, dukkha, and non-self.

Later meditation systems should not be projected wholesale backward.

Likewise the modern formula:

> "mindfulness = bare nonjudgmental present-moment awareness"

is historically too narrow to represent the entire early practice complex.

---

### EBTC-015 — Jhāna/concentration is an early practice

**Recoverability:** A/B

Structured absorptive/concentrative states are deeply embedded in early path descriptions.

Their exact phenomenology and sequence are disputed.

Their centrality to early Buddhist practice is much stronger than modern presentations that reduce the path to ordinary mindfulness alone.

---

### EBTC-016 — Awakening is presented as knowable transformation, not merely inherited belief

**Recoverability:** A/B

The tradition claims that liberation involves direct knowledge/seeing rather than mere assent to doctrine.

This makes early Buddhism a particularly important **illumination witness** under EPC-004.

The claim remains:

> disciplined practice can transform cognition such that the practitioner directly understands the processes producing suffering and release.

Historical study can establish that this was taught.

Whether the claimed final knowledge corresponds to ultimate metaphysical reality remains an epistemological question.

---

### EBTC-017 — Sacrificial or hereditary status is not sufficient for liberation

**Recoverability:** B

Early Buddhist discourses repeatedly relativize Brahmanical birth and ritual status in favor of:

- conduct;
- insight;
- discipline;
- and liberation.

This does **not** establish that early Buddhism abolished all social hierarchy or modern caste structures.

It does establish that spiritual attainment is not presented as the monopoly of hereditary Brahmins.

---

### EBTC-018 — Community and discipline matter

**Recoverability:** A/B

The Buddha movement rapidly formed an organized Saṅgha.

The path is therefore not purely solitary psychology.

It includes:

- teaching;
- communal discipline;
- renunciation;
- teacher-student relationships;
- lay-monastic exchange;
- and preservation of Dhamma/Vinaya.

Many detailed Vinaya rules are historically layered.

The existence and importance of disciplined community are early.

---

### EBTC-019 — Non-harming, goodwill, and compassion belong to the ethical field

**Recoverability:** B

Compassion becomes especially prominent in later Mahāyāna, but concern for non-harming, goodwill, sympathetic concern, and liberation from ill will is already present in early Buddhism.

The later universal bodhisattva ideal must not be projected wholesale into the earliest corpus.

---

### EBTC-020 — The Buddha is a discoverer/teacher of Dhamma rather than creator of reality

**Recoverability:** A/B

Early texts can describe conditionality/Dhamma as a reality or regularity that remains whether or not a Tathāgata appears; a Buddha awakens to it and teaches it.

This is crucial for comparative theology.

The early Buddhist architecture does not require:

> a creator God disclosing commands to a prophet.

Its paradigm is closer to:

> awakened discovery of how conditioned existence works and how liberation is achieved.

---

## 10. The Four Noble Truths

The Four Noble Truths are among the best-known Buddhist formulations:

1. dukkha;
2. its arising/origin;
3. cessation;
4. the path leading to cessation.

They are attested across Buddhist canonical traditions and remembered with notable stability.

However, this dossier distinguishes:

- **high confidence in the fourfold diagnostic structure**;
- from
- **lower confidence that every surviving literary elaboration of the first sermon reproduces one verbatim historical speech.**

The four truths are therefore foundational candidates as a **teaching architecture**, not necessarily as an exact transcript.

---

## 11. Non-self: strongest and weaker claims

FWSD-003 separates three levels.

### NS-1 — Strongly recoverable

The five aggregates are impermanent, afflicted/unstable, and not properly identified as a permanent controllable self.

### NS-2 — Strongly plausible

Attachment to an "I/me/mine" structure is a major contributor to suffering.

### NS-3 — Philosophically disputed

No enduring self, soul, subject, or personal identity exists in any ultimate sense whatsoever.

Some Buddhist philosophical traditions clearly defend strong versions of NS-3.

Whether every such later formulation can be attributed directly to Gautama is less certain.

This distinction is essential to the project's comparison with:

- ātman;
- soul;
- resurrection;
- continuity of consciousness;
- and personal identity.

---

## 12. Dependent arising: source-controlled meaning

The early formula:

> when this is, that comes to be; with the arising of this, that arises; when this ceases, that ceases

expresses conditionality.

In early contexts its main soteriological function is explaining:

- how suffering and rebirth arise;
- and how cessation becomes possible.

Later traditions extend dependent arising in sophisticated ontological directions.

Modern ecological "interdependence" discourse often extends it further still.

These developments may be valuable.

They shall not be silently identified with the earliest recoverable meaning.

---

## 13. Nibbāna / nirvāṇa

### 13.1 Recoverable core

Nibbāna is the soteriological goal of the early path.

It is associated with:

- cessation of craving;
- dispassion;
- release;
- destruction of greed, hatred, and delusion;
- freedom from the processes sustaining further birth;
- and peace.

### 13.2 Metaphysical dispute

The early texts use multiple modes of description.

Later interpreters debate whether nibbāna should be understood as:

- a metaphysical reality;
- an unconditioned element;
- a cessation state;
- a transformed mode of experience;
- or in other ways.

FWSD-003 shall not force one later school interpretation into the founder corpus.

---

## 14. Gods and creator-God question

Early Buddhism is **not simply atheism** in the modern Western sense.

Its textual world contains:

- devas;
- Brahmā beings;
- Māra;
- heavens;
- and multiple nonhuman beings.

However, these beings are generally themselves within conditioned existence and do not function as an omnipotent creator whose grace is necessary for liberation.

Thus:

> **early Buddhism is non-creator-centered, not simply devoid of supernatural ontology.**

This distinction is crucial for TCP-C001.

---

## 15. Awakening and revelation classification

Under EPC-004, the EBTC is primarily an:

## **I — Illumination Witness**

rather than an R-type prophetic dictation witness.

The dominant structure is:

```
human condition
      ↓
discipline / meditation / inquiry
      ↓
awakening / direct knowledge
      ↓
teaching of Dhamma
```

not:

```
personal creator
      ↓
verbal message
      ↓
prophet repeats divine speech
```

This difference is methodologically important and must be preserved rather than translated away.

## 16. Ethical review

### 16.1 Strengths

Strong ethically relevant features include:

- non-harming;
- reduction of hatred;
- truthfulness;
- restraint;
- generosity;
- compassion/goodwill;
- attention to intention;
- reduction of greed;
- and disciplined examination of one's own mental states.

### 16.2 Important cautions

The early corpus also belongs to an ancient social world and includes:

- monastic hierarchy;
- gendered rules;
- assumptions about kingship;
- ancient cosmology;
- and community structures that should not automatically become modern normative law.

### 16.3 Women and ordination

Early Buddhist traditions preserve a women's monastic order while also preserving narratives and rules that subordinate nuns institutionally in important ways.

The historical layering and school variation require independent review.

Neither:

> "Buddhism was simply egalitarian"

nor:

> "Buddhism simply excluded women"

adequately describes the source record.

### 16.4 Hard-stop review

No source-frozen core proposition identified above creates a corpus-level EPC-002 hard stop.

Specific disciplinary texts and socially contingent regulations require separate ethical evaluation.

---

## 17. Relation to prior Indian traditions

Early Buddhism developed within an Indian intellectual and renunciant environment already containing:

- karma;
- rebirth;
- asceticism;
- meditation;
- liberation;
- and competing ideas of self.

Therefore these elements cannot automatically be counted as independent Buddhist discoveries.

The high-value historical question is often:

> **How did early Buddhism transform inherited ideas?**

Examples include:

- recasting karma around intention;
- challenging permanent-self theories;
- rejecting extreme self-mortification;
- and making conditionality central to liberation analysis.

---

## 18. Independence analysis

### 18.1 Relative independence from Abrahamic and Iranian corpora

The early Buddhist teaching complex provides a major cross-cultural comparison point relatively independent of:

- Hebrew prophecy;
- early Christianity;
- and Islam.

Possible long-distance cultural contacts existed in ancient Eurasia, but the core Buddhist liberation structure does not require direct borrowing from those later corpora.

### 18.2 Reduced independence from South Asian traditions

Comparisons with:

- Vedic;
- Upaniṣadic;
- Jain;
- and other śramaṇa traditions

must account for shared environment, debate, and inherited concepts.

### 18.3 Later contact

Later Buddhist development in Central Asia interacted with:

- Iranian;
- Hellenistic;
- Hindu;
- and other traditions.

Later similarities cannot automatically be used as evidence for the founder-level corpus.

---

## 19. Contradiction map

### 19.1 Creator God

EBTC does not require a supreme creator God for liberation.

This remains a major tension with strict Abrahamic creator theology.

### 19.2 Permanent self

Non-self analysis stands in serious tension with traditions affirming an eternal individual soul or ātman.

The exact severity depends on how strongly non-self is formulated.

### 19.3 Rebirth versus resurrection

The early Buddhist worldview assumes repeated birth and death.

This conflicts, under ordinary literal interpretation, with one-life-then-resurrection models.

### 19.4 Grace versus disciplined cultivation

EBTC emphasizes disciplined practice and insight.

This need not logically exclude grace, but it differs from systems in which divine initiative is metaphysically necessary for salvation.

### 19.5 Cyclic versus linear time

Early Buddhist cosmology assumes repeated cycles rather than one unique linear creation-to-final-consummation history.

### 19.6 Divine-command ethics versus causal moral psychology

Early Buddhist ethics does not require moral obligation to be grounded in command from a creator deity.

This is a framework difference, though potentially reconcilable in constructive theology.

---

## 20. Comparative convergence map

Despite hard differences, the EBTC strongly converges at an abstract level with several other candidate traditions on:

- objective constraints imposed by reality rather than preference;
- human cognitive/moral distortion;
- self-centered craving as destructive;
- transformation rather than assent alone;
- ethical discipline;
- truthfulness;
- non-harming;
- consequences of action;
- and the inadequacy of merely external religious performance.

These convergences remain more evidentially interesting than forcing equivalence between:

- Dhamma and God;
- nibbāna and heaven;
- Buddha and prophet;
- karma and divine judgment;
- or awakening and revelation.

## 21. Textual and interpretive risk register

### RISK-BUD-001 — Pāli exclusivity

Treating the Pāli Canon as the only early source can mistake Theravāda recension history for original Buddhism.

**Mitigation:** systematic use of Āgama, Sanskrit, Gāndhārī, and Vinaya parallels.

### RISK-BUD-002 — Canon equals founder speech

A discourse can be canonical without being a verbatim historical sermon.

**Mitigation:** unit-level comparative reconstruction.

### RISK-BUD-003 — Hagiographic biography

Later Buddha-life narratives may be projected backward into history.

**Mitigation:** distinguish early biographical fragments from complete later biography.

### RISK-BUD-004 — Modern mindfulness retrojection

Modern therapeutic mindfulness may be mistaken for the whole early path.

**Mitigation:** preserve ethics, concentration, jhāna, renunciation, karma, rebirth, and liberation context.

### RISK-BUD-005 — Strong no-self overstatement

"Not-self" analysis may be converted without argument into a single total metaphysical proposition.

**Mitigation:** distinguish aggregate non-identification from later philosophical systematics.

### RISK-BUD-006 — Rebirth erasure

Modern naturalistic readings may omit rebirth because it is culturally uncomfortable.

**Mitigation:** distinguish historical presence from metaphysical acceptance.

### RISK-BUD-007 — Later emptiness back-projection

Madhyamaka śūnyatā may be read directly into every early discourse.

**Mitigation:** separate early dependent arising/non-self from later philosophical developments.

### RISK-BUD-008 — Universal egalitarianism

Criticism of hereditary religious privilege may be mistaken for a complete modern equality doctrine.

**Mitigation:** source-specific social analysis.

### RISK-BUD-009 — "Buddhism is atheism"

Absence of creator centrality may be mistaken for denial of all gods or supernatural beings.

**Mitigation:** preserve early Buddhist cosmological categories accurately.

### RISK-BUD-010 — Mystical infallibility

Awakening claims may be treated as self-validating metaphysical proof.

**Mitigation:** distinguish phenomenological transformation, claimed knowledge, and metaphysical truth under EPC-001.

## 22. Strongest case for foundational admission

The strongest cumulative case is:

1. **Historical founder probability:** a historical Gautama behind the movement is strongly supported.
2. **Cross-school recoverability:** many teachings recur in recensions belonging to later distinct Buddhist schools.
3. **Methodological richness:** textual discrepancies allow reconstruction rather than merely preventing it.
4. **Coherent practical system:** ethics, meditation, and insight form an integrated transformation path.
5. **Phenomenological seriousness:** the tradition subjects craving, attention, self-identification, and suffering to sustained disciplined analysis.
6. **Ethical value:** non-harming, truthfulness, restraint, goodwill, and reduction of greed/hatred are structurally central.
7. **Comparative independence:** the corpus provides an important non-Abrahamic, non-Iranian illumination witness.
8. **Corrective value:** inclusion prevents "revelation" from being defined constitutionally as only verbal messages from a creator deity.
9. **Falsifiable distinctions:** its metaphysical claims can be separated from phenomenological and practical claims rather than accepted as one block.
10. **Continuing testability:** aspects of meditation, attention, craving, and self-identification are at least partially available to disciplined experiential investigation.

---

## 23. Strongest case against foundational admission

The strongest objections are substantial:

1. **No contemporary writings:** nothing survives from Gautama's lifetime.
2. **Long oral transmission:** centuries separate the founder from the first major written redactions.
3. **Late surviving manuscripts:** extant manuscript evidence is still centuries removed.
4. **School plurality:** different Buddhist schools preserved different recensions.
5. **Creative composition:** oral literature could produce complete discourses from older formulaic material.
6. **Biography uncertainty:** much of the familiar Buddha life is hagiographic rather than secure history.
7. **Founder attribution variance:** some central teachings may be early community systematization rather than verbatim founder doctrine.
8. **Metaphysical burden:** rebirth, karma across lives, realms, and final release from rebirth are not established by historical method.
9. **Philosophical ambiguity:** non-self, nibbāna, dependent arising, consciousness, and personal continuity remain interpretively contested.
10. **Institutional development:** monastic disciplinary structures changed and should not be treated as one-time founder legislation.

These objections strongly oppose any doctrine of perfect verbal preservation.

They do not defeat the EBTC as a foundational-witness candidate.

## 26. Provisional recommendation

### 26.1 Procedural status

**Remain CAND-3 — Evaluated.**

### 26.2 Recommendation after first-cycle comparison

The Early Buddhist Teaching Corpus should be treated as a **serious candidate for CAND-4 — Foundational Candidate**.

If eventually admitted, the most defensible structure would likely distinguish:

### FWC-A1 candidate units

- dukkha and its causal analysis;
- cessation;
- disciplined path;
- ethical restraint;
- impermanence;
- dependent conditionality;
- non-self analysis;
- meditation/concentration;
- and the transformation of greed, hatred, and delusion.

### FWC-A2 / EWC-B candidate units

- more elaborate cosmological frameworks;
- detailed rebirth schemas;
- specific monastic rules;
- founder biography;
- and disputed metaphysical systematics.

This is only a provisional structural suggestion, not an admission decision.

### 26.3 What should not happen

Do not:

- canonize the entire Pāli Canon merely because early Buddhist teaching is foundational;
- pretend all Nikāya discourses are verbatim Gautama speech;
- erase rebirth from the historical corpus because modern readers dislike it;
- force acceptance of rebirth because it is historically early;
- translate Dhamma as "God";
- translate nibbāna as "heaven";
- identify Buddha as simply another prophet;
- equate non-self with modern nihilism;
- equate dependent arising with generic ecological interconnection;
- or project later Mahāyāna doctrines directly into the historical Buddha without source evidence.

## 29. Dossier conclusion

The Early Buddhist Teaching Corpus survives foundational-witness review strongly, but only when the historical candidate is distinguished from "Buddhism" as a whole.

The strongest recoverable early Buddhist witness is neither:

> a complete timeless Pāli Canon dictated verbatim by Gautama,

nor:

> a purely modern psychological system of meditation with karma, rebirth, renunciation, and cosmology removed.

A substantial early teaching architecture survives critical comparison:

- conditioned life is marked by dukkha;
- craving and ignorance contribute causally to that condition;
- cessation is possible;
- ethical discipline, concentration, mindfulness, and insight form a path;
- impermanent bodily and mental processes should not be grasped as a permanent controlling self;
- actions and intentions have consequences;
- attachment and hatred can be transformed;
- and awakening is presented as experiential knowledge rather than mere inherited assent.

For EPC-004 purposes, that makes the EBTC a major candidate for foundational reception.

Its greatest contribution to the developing corpus may be methodological as much as doctrinal:

> **it provides an early, sophisticated model of religious transformation that does not depend on a creator-God speaking through a prophet.**

Its greatest danger is the opposite of the Jesus dossier's danger.

With Jesus, later theology can be projected backward because devotion is strong.

With early Buddhism, modern secularization can be projected backward because contemporary readers prefer a naturalized meditation system.

FWSD-003 shall permit neither.
