# PREAMBLE

We establish this Foundational Religious Constitution in recognition that a religious community requires more than beliefs, leaders, property, or organizational machinery.

It requires an order by which truth may be sought, teachings may be preserved and developed, authority may be entrusted and restrained, communities may deliberate, leaders may serve, disagreements may be resolved, persons may be protected, and the work of one generation may pass faithfully to another.

We therefore reject the proposition that the religious community belongs to its founder, its leaders, its governing board, its clergy, its donors, any faction within it, or any other private person.

Authority exists in trust.

Office exists for service.

Leadership exists for the fulfillment of the religious purposes of the community.

No human officeholder is identical with the religion, the community, the truth it seeks, or the constitutional order under which the officeholder serves.

We further recognize that religious communities are vulnerable to distinctive forms of failure, including:

- domination disguised as spiritual authority;
- institutional preservation elevated above truth;
- charisma substituted for accountability;
- wealth substituted for wisdom;
- popularity substituted for discernment;
- bureaucracy substituted for mission;
- secrecy substituted for legitimate confidentiality;
- conformity substituted for unity;
- disagreement treated as disloyalty;
- religious discipline used to silence legitimate complaints;
- founding authority made permanent merely because it came first;
- and organizational power confused with religious truth.

Accordingly, this Constitution establishes a **Rule-Governed Conciliar Polity** in which authority is distributed according to competence, major religious questions are subject to conciliar discernment, leadership is accountable, institutional power is divided, and the entire constitutional order remains directed toward the religious purposes for which the community exists.

# ARTICLE I
## Nature and Purpose of This Constitution

### Section 1.1 — Supreme Ecclesiastical Instrument

This Constitution is the highest internal ecclesiastical governing instrument of the religious community associated with [[CORP_NAME]].

All subordinate ecclesiastical constitutions, councils, offices, codes, rules, doctrines, policies, covenants, procedures, decisions, and religious institutions shall conform to it.

No ecclesiastical act inconsistent with this Constitution shall possess authority merely because it was undertaken by a senior leader, council, founder, majority, board, or longstanding custom.

### Section 1.2 — Relationship to Civil Law

This Constitution establishes the internal religious order of the community.

It does not purport to repeal, evade, or supersede civil law where that law validly applies.

The Corporation's Charter, civil bylaws, and other civil governing instruments shall govern matters within their lawful domain.

Where religious and civil authority intersect, the governing instruments shall be interpreted, to the fullest extent reasonably and lawfully possible, so that:

1. the religious identity and autonomy of the community are preserved;
2. the Corporation remains lawfully administered;
3. charitable assets remain protected;
4. ecclesiastical questions remain within ecclesiastical jurisdiction where applicable;
5. fiduciary matters remain subject to proper fiduciary oversight; and
6. neither civil nor ecclesiastical office is casually allowed to absorb the proper jurisdiction of the other.

### Section 1.3 — Religious Rather Than Proprietary Order

No person has an ownership interest in the religion, religious community, religious offices, doctrine, ministry, goodwill, charitable assets, or institutional identity merely by reason of:

- founding the organization;
- holding office;
- providing financial support;
- serving as clergy;
- being a member;
- creating intellectual works;
- exercising influence;
- or having participated in the community for any period of time.

Any legally protectable property interest shall be determined by applicable law and valid agreements rather than by ecclesiastical status alone.

### Section 1.4 — Purpose of Governance

Governance exists to enable and protect the religious mission.

Governance is not itself the mission.

No governing institution should acquire powers merely because additional power would make governance more convenient.

# ARTICLE II
## Foundational Constitutional Principles

The following principles govern the interpretation and development of the entire ecclesiastical order.

### Section 2.1 — Truth Above Institution

The preservation of an institution shall never be treated as more important than fidelity to truth.

No teaching shall be maintained merely because acknowledging error would embarrass the institution or its leaders.

### Section 2.2 — Rule Above Officeholder

Every human office exists under the governing order.

No officeholder may lawfully claim exemption from constitutional limits merely by reason of seniority, sacred office, charisma, founding status, spiritual gifts, religious experience, or institutional necessity.

### Section 2.3 — Authority According to Competence

Authority shall be allocated according to the nature of the decision to be made.

Religious teaching authority, spiritual leadership, fiduciary administration, operational management, constitutional interpretation, investigation, and adjudication are distinct functions.

Possession of one form of authority shall not automatically confer another.

### Section 2.4 — Service Rather Than Domination

Religious authority shall be understood as stewardship and service.

Leadership shall not be used to:

- demand personal loyalty in place of fidelity to the community's principles;
- suppress legitimate questions;
- obtain improper financial or personal benefit;
- control persons beyond the legitimate scope of the religious relationship;
- compel concealment of misconduct;
- or make another person's access to the sacred dependent upon personal obedience to an officeholder.

### Section 2.5 — Conciliarity

Important religious decisions should ordinarily be discerned through competent councils rather than unilateral decree.

Conciliarity does not eliminate leadership.

Leadership does not eliminate conciliarity.

### Section 2.6 — Discernment Before Majoritarianism

Voting is a governance mechanism, not a method for determining truth.

Before significant religious decisions are resolved by vote, the responsible body shall seek, according to applicable procedures, to understand:

- the actual question;
- the relevant sources;
- competing interpretations;
- relevant evidence;
- foreseeable consequences;
- minority concerns;
- and the possibility of a more faithful resolution.

### Section 2.7 — Subsidiarity

A matter should ordinarily be resolved at the lowest level competent to resolve it responsibly.

Higher authority exists to serve functions that lower levels cannot adequately perform, including:

- maintaining common doctrine;
- preserving shared standards;
- hearing appeals;
- resolving intercommunity disputes;
- protecting persons where local structures fail;
- and coordinating matters genuinely common to the broader religious body.

### Section 2.8 — Accountability

Every office entrusted with meaningful authority shall be subject to an accountability mechanism proportionate to that authority.

The greater the power, the stronger the required accountability.

### Section 2.9 — Due Process

Serious adverse action against a person should ordinarily require:

- fair notice;
- specification of the matter at issue;
- meaningful opportunity to respond;
- impartial consideration;
- application of known standards;
- a reasoned determination where appropriate;
- and review or appeal where this Constitution or subordinate law provides it.

### Section 2.10 — Institutional Continuity

The religious order shall be designed to survive its founders and current leaders.

No essential institutional function should depend permanently upon the continued presence of one natural person.

### Section 2.11 — Integrity of Process

A desirable result does not excuse an invalid process.

Good ends shall not be used routinely to justify unconstitutional means.

### Section 2.12 — Human Dignity

Every person shall be treated as possessing inherent dignity independent of:

- rank;
- wealth;
- office;
- membership status;
- religious knowledge;
- institutional usefulness;
- disagreement;
- or personal relationship with leadership.

### Section 2.13 — Freedom of Conscience

Religious belonging necessarily involves commitments and may legitimately involve standards of belief and conduct.

Nevertheless, spiritual authority shall not be construed as ownership of another person's conscience.

Persons shall remain free to:

- ask good-faith questions;
- seek understanding;
- disclose uncertainty;
- decline religious office;
- withdraw from voluntary religious membership;
- report wrongdoing;
- and seek lawful outside assistance.

This provision does not require the religious community to treat every belief as compatible with its doctrine.

### Section 2.14 — Protection of the Vulnerable

The protection of children, vulnerable adults, persons receiving pastoral care, and persons subject to substantial spiritual or institutional power is a foundational obligation.

No claim of tradition, confidentiality, hierarchy, forgiveness, reconciliation, reputation, or religious autonomy shall itself justify exposing a vulnerable person to preventable abuse.

### Section 2.15 — No Retaliation for Good-Faith Reporting

No person shall suffer religious or institutional retaliation merely for making a good-faith report concerning:

- abuse;
- exploitation;
- unlawful conduct;
- serious ethical misconduct;
- financial impropriety;
- violation of safeguarding rules;
- or substantial abuse of authority.

Knowingly false accusations may themselves be subject to appropriate process.

### Section 2.16 — Correction

The religious community shall remain capable of admitting institutional, doctrinal, interpretive, administrative, and personal error.

Correction undertaken through valid process shall not itself be regarded as institutional weakness.

# ARTICLE III
## Religious Identity and Foundational Purpose

### Section 3.1 — Religious Character

The community governed by this Constitution is religious in nature and purpose.

Its activities may include worship, spiritual practice, education, contemplation, scholarship, service, charity, community life, publication, formation, pastoral care, cultural expression, and other activities undertaken in furtherance of its religious purposes.

### Section 3.2 — Foundational Religious Purpose

The religious community exists to seek, understand, embody, preserve, develop, teach, practice, and transmit the religious truths and way of life recognized through its duly established religious sources and processes.

### Section 3.3 — Mission

The mission of the religious community shall include, without limitation:

1. cultivation of spiritual life;
2. pursuit of truth and wisdom;
3. formation of persons and communities;
4. teaching and transmission of religious understanding;
5. development of ethical character;
6. practice of compassion, service, and benevolence;
7. establishment and support of healthy religious communities;
8. preservation and responsible development of religious teachings and practices;
9. preparation and accountability of religious leaders;
10. service to humanity consistent with the community's religious principles.

### Section 3.4 — Specific Theology Reserved

This Constitution establishes the governing framework within which the religion's substantive theology shall be articulated.

It does not, merely by establishing governance, determine specific propositions regarding:

- God or ultimate reality;
- revelation;
- cosmology;
- human nature;
- salvation, liberation, transformation, or spiritual fulfillment;
- sacred texts;
- prophets or teachers;
- metaphysics;
- ritual;
- afterlife;
- or any other substantive doctrinal question.

Such matters shall be addressed through duly adopted foundational principles, doctrine, authoritative interpretations, and religious teaching.

# ARTICLE IV
## Sources of Religious Authority

### Section 4.1 — Need for Defined Sources

The religious community shall expressly identify the sources upon which authoritative religious judgments may rely.

No leader or council may treat personal preference as authoritative merely by describing it as religious.

### Section 4.2 — Source Categories

The Ecclesiastical Constitution or a duly adopted Foundational Sources Instrument shall identify the role, weight, and relationship of sources that may include:

1. foundational religious principles;
2. sacred or authoritative texts;
3. teachings attributed to recognized founders, prophets, sages, teachers, or exemplars;
4. received religious tradition;
5. reason and rational inquiry;
6. moral reasoning;
7. spiritual experience;
8. contemplative insight;
9. empirical knowledge;
10. historical scholarship;
11. accumulated community wisdom;
12. conscience;
13. authoritative precedents;
14. other duly recognized sources.

Inclusion in this list does not establish equal weight among sources.

### Section 4.3 — Source Hierarchy

The religious order shall eventually establish how conflicts among recognized sources are resolved.

Such hierarchy shall not be inferred merely from the institutional power of the person citing a source.

### Section 4.4 — Honest Use of Sources

No governing authority shall knowingly:

- fabricate a source;
- materially misrepresent a source;
- conceal decisive contrary evidence during formal doctrinal proceedings;
- attribute personal teachings falsely to an authoritative source;
- or alter an authoritative record deceptively.

### Section 4.5 — Historical and Evidentiary Humility

Where historical evidence is uncertain, disputed, incomplete, or capable of multiple reasonable interpretations, authoritative teaching should distinguish established fact from:

- inference;
- tradition;
- hypothesis;
- theological interpretation;
- symbolic interpretation;
- and uncertainty.

# ARTICLE V
## Hierarchy of Religious Teaching

### Section 5.1 — Classification Required

Religious propositions shall be classified according to their authority.

Not every sermon, lesson, commentary, council statement, founder statement, pastoral recommendation, or traditional practice constitutes doctrine.

### Section 5.2 — Level I: Foundational Principles

Foundational Principles define the essential religious identity of the community.

They shall receive the greatest constitutional protection.

### Section 5.3 — Level II: Formal Doctrine

Formal Doctrine consists of teachings adopted as authoritative through the constitutionally prescribed doctrinal process.

Formal Doctrine may develop through valid constitutional procedures.

### Section 5.4 — Level III: Authoritative Interpretation

Authoritative Interpretations explain the meaning or application of Foundational Principles or Formal Doctrine.

They are subordinate to the authorities they interpret.

### Section 5.5 — Level IV: Religious Practice and Discipline

This level includes authorized forms of:

- worship;
- ritual;
- spiritual practice;
- communal discipline;
- religious formation;
- ministerial practice;
- observance;
- and practical religious order.

These may adapt more readily than Formal Doctrine.

### Section 5.6 — Level V: Theological and Pastoral Opinion

Responsible theological speculation, personal teaching, pastoral judgment, and scholarly interpretation may exist without becoming binding doctrine.

### Section 5.7 — Classification Presumption

Where an authoritative classification has not been made, a statement shall not be treated as Foundational Principle or Formal Doctrine merely because it was made by a senior leader.

### Section 5.8 — Development Without Casual Mutation

The religious community shall preserve both:

- continuity sufficient for identity;
- and capacity for responsible correction and development.

Neither immobility nor continual reinvention shall be treated as an absolute virtue.

# ARTICLE VI
## Constitutional Authority and Religious Office

### Section 6.1 — Derived Authority

Every religious office possesses only authority derived from:

- this Constitution;
- valid subordinate governing instruments;
- or a lawful delegation made under them.

### Section 6.2 — No Inherent Unlimited Authority

No religious title creates unlimited jurisdiction.

### Section 6.3 — Office and Person Distinguished

Authority belongs to an office or constitutional function rather than to the personal identity of its occupant.

When a person leaves an office, the powers of that office do not ordinarily remain with that person.

### Section 6.4 — Qualification

Important religious authority shall be entrusted only to persons satisfying constitutionally established qualifications appropriate to the office.

Qualifications may include:

- knowledge;
- formation;
- character;
- experience;
- commitment to the religious tradition;
- capacity for discernment;
- ability to work conciliarity;
- safeguarding fitness;
- and freedom from disqualifying conflicts.

### Section 6.5 — Fiduciary and Spiritual Authority Distinguished

Religious qualification does not itself establish qualification to manage money, property, employment, legal affairs, or corporate operations.

Likewise, professional or fiduciary qualification does not itself confer authority to determine religious doctrine.

# ARTICLE VII
## Conciliar Governance

### Section 7.1 — General Principle

Major religious authority shall ordinarily be exercised through a combination of responsible leadership and conciliar deliberation.

### Section 7.2 — Councils

Subordinate constitutions may establish councils possessing:

- deliberative;
- legislative;
- doctrinal;
- advisory;
- supervisory;
- adjudicative;
- appellate;
- or other defined authority.

No council shall possess powers merely because it exists.

### Section 7.3 — Presiding Leadership

A presiding religious leader may:

- convene;
- guide;
- teach;
- frame questions;
- facilitate deliberation;
- seek unity;
- articulate proposed conclusions;
- and exercise specifically delegated executive authority.

Presiding does not mean possessing the votes or consciences of other council members.

### Section 7.4 — Duty of Independent Judgment

Persons participating in religious councils shall exercise independent judgment consistent with their duties.

They shall not be bound merely to ratify the wishes of:

- the founder;
- the Presiding Spiritual Leader;
- donors;
- the civil Board;
- political factions;
- or other persons lacking authority to command their judgment.

### Section 7.5 — Minority Responsibility

Dissent expressed in good faith shall be heard and fairly considered.

A minority position does not become correct because it is a minority position, but neither does a majority position become true merely because it commands more votes.

### Section 7.6 — Written Reasons

Major doctrinal, constitutional, disciplinary, and appellate decisions should ordinarily be accompanied by a record adequate to identify:

- the issue;
- the authority exercised;
- the governing sources;
- the decision;
- and the reasoning necessary to understand it.

Confidential information may be protected appropriately.

# ARTICLE VIII
## Discernment and Decision-Making

### Section 8.1 — Discernment

Significant religious decisions shall ordinarily seek understanding before resolution.

### Section 8.2 — Required Elements

Where appropriate, discernment shall include:

1. defining the question;
2. identifying jurisdiction;
3. identifying applicable religious sources;
4. gathering relevant evidence;
5. hearing materially different positions;
6. examining foreseeable consequences;
7. disclosing conflicts;
8. permitting reflection;
9. seeking a common judgment;
10. determining whether further delay is beneficial or harmful.

### Section 8.3 — Consensus and Unity

Substantial unity is preferred where reasonably attainable.

Consensus shall not mean that every participant possesses an unlimited veto.

### Section 8.4 — Formal Vote

Where discernment does not produce sufficient unity and a decision is necessary, the competent body may decide through the applicable voting threshold.

### Section 8.5 — Higher Thresholds

The importance and irreversibility of a decision shall generally increase the required threshold and procedural safeguards.

### Section 8.6 — No Manufactured Unity

Leaders shall not manufacture apparent consensus through:

- intimidation;
- retaliation;
- selective exclusion;
- withholding material information;
- spiritual threats;
- artificial urgency;
- or suppression of legitimate dissent.

# ARTICLE IX
## Civil and Ecclesiastical Domains

### Section 9.1 — Dual Constitutional Order

The religious community recognizes both:

- an ecclesiastical order;
- and a civil corporate order.

Each possesses legitimate but limited authority.

### Section 9.2 — Ecclesiastical Jurisdiction

Matters presumptively ecclesiastical include:

- doctrine;
- theology;
- religious teaching;
- worship;
- ritual;
- religious practice;
- ministerial religious qualification;
- spiritual formation;
- ecclesiastical discipline;
- religious membership;
- religious offices;
- authoritative religious interpretation.

### Section 9.3 — Civil and Fiduciary Jurisdiction

Matters presumptively civil or fiduciary include:

- banking;
- accounting;
- corporate records;
- regulatory filings;
- tax administration;
- insurance;
- contracts;
- secular legal compliance;
- fiduciary controls;
- financial reporting;
- title to corporate property.

### Section 9.4 — Concurrent Jurisdiction

Some matters inherently involve both domains.

These may include:

- appointment and employment of senior religious leaders;
- compensation of senior religious leaders;
- establishment or closure of major institutions;
- acquisition or disposition of major religious property;
- affiliation;
- disaffiliation;
- merger;
- dissolution;
- and governing-document amendments affecting both domains.

Concurrent matters require the approvals established by subordinate constitutional law.

### Section 9.5 — Neither Domain May Manufacture Jurisdiction

No civil authority may convert a theological question into a fiduciary question merely to acquire control over it.

No ecclesiastical authority may convert an ordinary financial or legal question into a spiritual question merely to avoid legitimate accountability.

# ARTICLE X
## Religious Community and Belonging

### Section 10.1 — Community

The religious community consists more broadly than its formal members.

Persons may participate at different levels of relationship and commitment.

### Section 10.2 — Graded Affiliation

Subordinate law may recognize categories including:

- visitor;
- participant;
- adherent;
- covenant member;
- minister;
- religious officer;
- affiliated community;
- or other appropriate categories.

### Section 10.3 — No Hierarchy of Human Worth

Differences in religious office, formation, or membership status establish differences of function or relationship, not differences in inherent human dignity.

### Section 10.4 — Covenant Membership

Formal religious membership may involve:

- affirmation of defined commitments;
- participation in community life;
- rights of consultation;
- eligibility for specified responsibilities;
- accountability to religious standards;
- and access to defined processes.

### Section 10.5 — Corporate Membership Distinguished

Religious or ecclesiastical membership shall not itself constitute statutory membership in the associated nonprofit corporation unless the civil Charter expressly provides otherwise.

### Section 10.6 — Withdrawal

Except where a lawful obligation independently exists, participation in religious membership shall remain voluntary.

A person may withdraw from religious membership according to procedures established by subordinate law.

Withdrawal shall not be obstructed through spiritual coercion, threats, or improper withholding of property belonging to the withdrawing person.

# ARTICLE XI
## Rights Within the Religious Order

Subject to appropriate distinctions between members, ministers, officers, participants, and other persons, the religious constitutional order shall protect the following principles.

### Section 11.1 — Fair Process

Persons subject to serious ecclesiastical discipline shall receive fair process appropriate to the nature of the matter.

### Section 11.2 — Good-Faith Inquiry

Good-faith questions, intellectual inquiry, requests for clarification, and respectful disagreement shall not alone constitute misconduct.

### Section 11.3 — Access to Grievance Procedures

Persons substantially affected by the conduct of religious leadership shall have access to an appropriate grievance or reporting mechanism.

### Section 11.4 — Reporting Wrongdoing

No internal rule shall prohibit a person from lawfully reporting suspected:

- abuse;
- violence;
- exploitation;
- fraud;
- criminal conduct;
- or other wrongdoing

to appropriate civil authorities.

### Section 11.5 — Freedom From Retaliation

Good-faith use of authorized complaint, safeguarding, reporting, or appellate mechanisms shall not itself constitute disloyalty.

### Section 11.6 — Impartial Adjudication

A person with a material conflict shall not adjudicate a matter where impartiality is reasonably compromised.

### Section 11.7 — Appeal

Serious ecclesiastical sanctions should ordinarily be subject to meaningful review.

### Section 11.8 — Confidentiality

Persons shall receive reasonable protection for confidential information entrusted in circumstances where confidentiality is appropriate.

Confidentiality shall not be falsely promised where disclosure is legally required or reasonably necessary to prevent serious harm.

### Section 11.9 — Protection From Spiritual Exploitation

No person exercising religious authority may knowingly use that authority to obtain improper:

- sexual;
- romantic;
- financial;
- labor;
- political;
- or other personal benefit.

### Section 11.10 — No Purchase of Religious Authority

Donations or financial power shall not purchase:

- doctrinal authority;
- ministerial qualification;
- disciplinary immunity;
- religious office;
- or privileged access to spiritual status.

# ARTICLE XII
## Safeguarding

### Section 12.1 — Foundational Status

Safeguarding is not merely an administrative policy.

It is a constitutional responsibility.

### Section 12.2 — Children and Vulnerable Persons

The religious order shall maintain safeguards appropriate to activities involving:

- minors;
- vulnerable adults;
- pastoral counseling;
- residential communities;
- spiritual direction;
- intimate or confidential settings;
- and significant imbalances of institutional power.

### Section 12.3 — Mandatory Civil Duties

Nothing in ecclesiastical discipline, confession, reconciliation, mediation, pastoral care, or internal investigation shall be interpreted to prevent compliance with legally applicable reporting duties.

### Section 12.4 — Independence

Serious allegations involving senior leadership shall not be investigated solely by persons subordinate to the accused.

### Section 12.5 — No Forced Reconciliation

Forgiveness, compassion, reconciliation, restoration, or religious unity shall not require:

- a victim to resume contact with an alleged wrongdoer;
- concealment of misconduct;
- withdrawal of a truthful report;
- abandonment of lawful remedies;
- or restoration of an unsafe person to authority.

# ARTICLE XIII
## Discipline, Accountability, and Restoration

### Section 13.1 — Purposes of Discipline

Ecclesiastical discipline may serve:

- protection;
- truth;
- accountability;
- correction;
- restoration where possible;
- reconciliation where appropriate;
- preservation of religious integrity;
- and protection of the community.

### Section 13.2 — Proportionality

Sanctions should be proportionate to:

- seriousness;
- responsibility;
- harm;
- persistence;
- abuse of authority;
- likelihood of recurrence;
- and other relevant circumstances.

### Section 13.3 — Different Matters Require Different Procedures

The religious order shall distinguish among:

- interpersonal disagreement;
- theological disagreement;
- violations of community expectations;
- serious ethical misconduct;
- ministerial misconduct;
- safeguarding allegations;
- financial misconduct;
- criminal allegations.

### Section 13.4 — Restoration Distinguished From Reinstatement

Forgiveness or personal restoration shall not automatically entitle a person to return to religious office.

Some misconduct may permanently disqualify a person from particular forms of authority even where reconciliation is otherwise possible.

### Section 13.5 — Senior Leadership

The higher the office, the less permissible it is for that officeholder to control proceedings concerning their own alleged misconduct.

# ARTICLE XIV
## Founder and Founding Period

### Section 14.1 — Founding Role Recognized

A founder may possess an unusually important role in articulating the original religious vision, assembling the initial institutions, and establishing the first constitutional order.

This reality may be recognized openly rather than disguised.

### Section 14.2 — Founder Is Not Owner

Founding does not create perpetual ownership of:

- the religion;
- the religious community;
- the charitable corporation;
- its assets;
- or the consciences of its participants.

### Section 14.3 — Founding Period

Subordinate constitutional law may establish a defined Founding Period during which specified powers are temporarily entrusted to the Founder.

### Section 14.4 — Founder Powers Must Be Express

No special founder power exists merely by implication.

Any special founder authority shall be:

- specifically stated;
- limited in subject;
- limited in duration or termination condition;
- subject to safeguarding;
- and incapable of defeating the protected principles of this Constitution.

### Section 14.5 — Transition

The Founding Period shall be designed to lead toward a mature constitutional order capable of functioning without the Founder.

### Section 14.6 — Founder Accountability

No Founder shall be immune from:

- ethical standards;
- safeguarding requirements;
- conflict-of-interest requirements;
- disciplinary jurisdiction;
- removal from offices held;
- or lawful civil accountability.

# ARTICLE XV
## Leadership and Succession

### Section 15.1 — Offices Shall Outlive Officeholders

Religious continuity shall be rooted in principles, community, teaching, institutions, and valid succession rather than personal attachment to a single leader.

### Section 15.2 — Selection

Senior leaders shall ordinarily be selected through a process considering:

- spiritual qualification;
- knowledge;
- character;
- experience;
- demonstrated judgment;
- community trust;
- ability to serve within constitutional limits;
- and other requirements of office.

### Section 15.3 — No Automatic Hereditary Succession

No office shall pass through biological descent unless a future constitutional amendment expressly establishes such a rule.

### Section 15.4 — No Unilateral Successor Creation

A current leader may nominate, recommend, mentor, prepare, or identify potential successors where authorized.

No incumbent shall possess unilateral power to make a successor irrevocably binding upon the community unless this Constitution is lawfully amended to provide otherwise.

### Section 15.5 — Predetermined Succession

Every indispensable office shall have:

- vacancy rules;
- interim authority;
- succession procedure;
- installation procedure;
- and incapacity provisions.

### Section 15.6 — Peaceful Transfer

No leader shall obstruct a valid transfer of authority upon:

- expiration of term;
- resignation;
- incapacity;
- removal;
- or other constitutionally recognized vacancy.

# ARTICLE XVI
## Constitutional Guardianship

### Section 16.1 — Need for Constitutional Review

Neither the civil Board nor the principal ecclesiastical leadership shall possess unlimited authority to determine the extent of its own constitutional powers.

### Section 16.2 — Constitutional Guardians

The ecclesiastical order shall establish an institution capable of resolving constitutional questions impartially.

Its eventual name, composition, and procedure shall be established in subordinate constitutional law.

### Section 16.3 — Jurisdiction

Constitutional review may include:

- interpretation of this Constitution;
- jurisdictional disputes;
- validity of constitutional amendments;
- procedural validity;
- conflicts among governing instruments;
- separation of civil and ecclesiastical functions;
- and whether an organ exceeded its delegated authority.

### Section 16.4 — Doctrinal Restraint

The constitutional-review body shall not ordinarily determine substantive religious doctrine merely because a doctrinal question appears in a dispute.

Doctrine remains subject to the competent ecclesiastical authorities.

### Section 16.5 — No Political Supremacy

Constitutional Guardians shall not become a general governing council.

Their authority shall remain principally interpretive and jurisdictional.

# ARTICLE XVII
## Governance-Act Validity

### Section 17.1 — Validity Required

An ecclesiastical act possesses institutional authority only when made in substantial compliance with the constitutional requirements applicable to that act.

### Section 17.2 — Elements

Applicable validity requirements may include:

1. jurisdiction;
2. lawful composition;
3. qualification;
4. notice;
5. quorum;
6. absence or management of disqualifying conflicts;
7. required deliberation;
8. required voting threshold;
9. required concurrence;
10. proper record;
11. substantive constitutional authority.

### Section 17.3 — Void and Voidable Acts

Subordinate law may distinguish:

- acts void from inception;
- acts voidable upon challenge;
- curable procedural defects;
- and defects capable of ratification.

### Section 17.4 — No Validation by Power Alone

An otherwise invalid act shall not become valid solely because:

- it was ordered by a senior leader;
- it benefited the institution;
- it went unchallenged for some period;
- or a majority later found it convenient.

# ARTICLE XVIII
## Community Participation

### Section 18.1 — Community Is Not Merely an Audience

The religious community shall be capable of meaningful participation in its common life.

### Section 18.2 — Forms of Participation

Depending upon status and subject matter, participation may include:

- consultation;
- testimony;
- deliberation;
- petitions;
- nominations;
- objections;
- assemblies;
- leadership affirmation;
- review of reports;
- participation in communal discernment;
- and constitutionally reserved approvals.

### Section 18.3 — Participation Is Not Administration

Community participation does not mean every operational question must be resolved by general vote.

### Section 18.4 — Reserved Powers

The Ecclesiastical Constitution may reserve extraordinary decisions to broader community concurrence.

Candidates include:

- fundamental constitutional change;
- dissolution;
- certain mergers or affiliations;
- disposition of principal sacred property;
- extraordinary changes to religious identity;
- or confirmation of particular leadership transitions.

### Section 18.5 — No Manufactured Electorate

Leadership shall not manipulate membership status for the primary purpose of creating an artificial voting or consultation outcome.

---

# ARTICLE XIX
## Local Communities and Subsidiarity

### Section 19.1 — Local Religious Life

Where multiple communities exist, each should possess meaningful responsibility for its ordinary religious life.

### Section 19.2 — Shared Order

Local communities remain subject to common constitutional principles where affiliated within the same ecclesiastical body.

### Section 19.3 — Higher Intervention

Higher bodies shall not intervene in local affairs merely because they would have chosen differently.

Intervention should require a legitimate basis such as:

- doctrinal integrity;
- appeal;
- safeguarding;
- serious misconduct;
- intercommunity conflict;
- common property;
- constitutional violation;
- or another reserved matter.

### Section 19.4 — Future Scalability

The ecclesiastical system may develop:

> Local Community  
> → Regional Council  
> → General Ecclesiastical Council

or another constitutionally authorized structure as growth requires.

No unnecessary level of bureaucracy shall be created merely for symbolic prestige.

---

# ARTICLE XX
## Property, Finance, and Material Stewardship

### Section 20.1 — Religious Assets Are Held in Stewardship

Charitable and religious assets shall be administered for the purposes to which they are lawfully dedicated.

### Section 20.2 — Spiritual Office Does Not Create Ownership

Religious leadership does not create personal ownership of corporate property.

### Section 20.3 — Financial Accountability

Spiritual stature shall never excuse a person from reasonable:

- accounting;
- authorization;
- conflict-of-interest;
- compensation;
- reimbursement;
- contracting;
- and audit or review controls.

### Section 20.4 — Donor Influence

Restricted gifts may lawfully carry restrictions consistent with the mission and applicable law.

A donor shall not thereby acquire doctrinal authority or ownership of the religious institution.

### Section 20.5 — Major Sacred Property

The acquisition, encumbrance, alienation, or closure of property central to the religious life of the community may be designated a matter of concurrent civil and ecclesiastical jurisdiction.

---

# ARTICLE XXI
## Transparency, Confidentiality, and Records

### Section 21.1 — Presumption of Responsible Transparency

Governance shall be sufficiently transparent to permit legitimate accountability.

### Section 21.2 — Legitimate Confidentiality

Transparency does not require disclosure of information whose confidentiality protects legitimate interests, including:

- pastoral information;
- personal data;
- safeguarding matters;
- personnel matters;
- privileged legal communications;
- security matters;
- sensitive negotiations.

### Section 21.3 — Secrecy Distinguished From Confidentiality

Confidentiality protects legitimate interests.

Secrecy used primarily to prevent accountability does not acquire legitimacy merely by being labeled confidential.

### Section 21.4 — Constitutional Records

Major constitutional, doctrinal, disciplinary, and governance actions shall be preserved in records sufficient to establish their validity and institutional history.

### Section 21.5 — Historical Integrity

Official records shall not knowingly be altered to create a materially false account of institutional history.

Corrections shall be traceable where appropriate.

---

# ARTICLE XXII
## Conflict of Interest and Recusal

### Section 22.1 — Duty to Disclose

Persons exercising governance authority shall disclose material conflicts relevant to decisions in which they participate.

### Section 22.2 — Recusal

A person shall recuse where required by governing law or where the person's interest creates a substantial threat to impartial judgment.

### Section 22.3 — Seniority Does Not Cure Conflict

No leader is exempt from recusal merely by holding the highest office.

### Section 22.4 — Relational Conflicts

Subordinate law may require recusal based on:

- close family relationship;
- significant financial relationship;
- supervisory relationship;
- personal involvement;
- prior adjudicative role;
- or other circumstances reasonably affecting impartiality.

---

# ARTICLE XXIII
## Emergency Authority

### Section 23.1 — Limited Purpose

Emergency authority exists only to preserve the community's ability to respond to extraordinary circumstances.

### Section 23.2 — Temporary Character

Emergency powers shall:

- be defined;
- be recorded;
- be reviewable;
- be limited in scope;
- and expire automatically unless renewed through valid procedure.

### Section 23.3 — Protected Matters

Emergency authority shall not ordinarily permit:

- permanent alteration of Foundational Principles;
- abolition of constitutional oversight;
- permanent extension of an incumbent's office;
- transfer of charitable assets to insiders;
- destruction of disciplinary accountability;
- or permanent constitutional reorganization.

### Section 23.4 — Restoration of Normal Governance

Ordinary constitutional governance shall resume as soon as reasonably practicable.

---

# ARTICLE XXIV
## Schism, Affiliation, and Institutional Continuity

### Section 24.1 — Disagreement Anticipated

The Constitution recognizes that serious disagreement may arise without presuming that every disagreement constitutes disloyalty.

### Section 24.2 — Peaceful Processes

Subordinate law should establish peaceful procedures for:

- internal disagreement;
- mediation;
- appeal;
- affiliation;
- disaffiliation;
- separation of communities;
- and institutional reorganization.

### Section 24.3 — Charitable Property

Charitable property shall not become personal or factional property merely because one group prevails in an internal controversy.

### Section 24.4 — Religious Identity

Procedures governing institutional separation should protect:

- continuity of charitable purpose;
- legitimate donor restrictions;
- religious identity;
- rights established under governing instruments;
- and lawful civil ownership.

### Section 24.5 — No Violence or Intimidation

No internal religious dispute legitimizes violence, threats, harassment, destruction of property, or unlawful coercion.

---

# ARTICLE XXV
## Amendment and Constitutional Development

### Section 25.1 — Constitution May Develop

This Constitution is intended to endure without assuming that the founding generation possesses perfect foresight.

It may therefore be amended through extraordinary constitutional process.

### Section 25.2 — Amendment Is Not Ordinary Legislation

This Constitution shall not be amended merely because compliance with it is inconvenient.

### Section 25.3 — Amendment Requirements

The Ecclesiastical Constitution shall establish an amendment procedure requiring, at minimum:

1. a written proposal;
2. identification of affected provisions;
3. explanation of reasons;
4. adequate notice;
5. meaningful period for study and discernment;
6. consideration by the competent ecclesiastical authority;
7. extraordinary approval threshold;
8. any required community concurrence;
9. constitutional validity review;
10. permanent recording of the amendment.

### Section 25.4 — Entrenched Provisions

Certain provisions may be designated **Entrenched**.

Entrenched provisions shall require a process more demanding than ordinary constitutional amendment.

### Section 25.5 — Fundamental Refounding

A change so extensive that it would create a religious order materially different in fundamental identity may require an extraordinary **Refounding Process** rather than an ordinary amendment.

### Section 25.6 — No Implied Amendment

Custom, nonenforcement, contrary practice, leader instruction, or repeated violation shall not by itself amend this Constitution.

# ARTICLE XXVI
## Entrenched Constitutional Commitments

The following principles are provisionally designated for special protection and shall not be overridden by ordinary ecclesiastical legislation:

1. no human office possesses unlimited authority;
2. religious authority exists in trust and for service;
3. no leader possesses final jurisdiction over serious allegations against themselves;
4. charitable or religious assets do not become private property through religious office;
5. good-faith reporting of serious wrongdoing may not be prohibited by religious discipline;
6. no person may purchase religious authority through financial contribution;
7. governance functions shall remain subject to constitutional jurisdiction;
8. Founder status does not create perpetual ownership of the institution;
9. succession shall occur through constitutional process rather than seizure of authority;
10. significant religious authority shall remain accountable;
11. children and vulnerable persons are entitled to institutional safeguarding;
12. the civil Board shall not acquire doctrinal authority merely through corporate office;
13. ecclesiastical leadership shall not escape legitimate fiduciary controls merely through religious office;
14. constitutional amendment shall require more than ordinary executive action;
15. persons exercising adjudicative authority shall be subject to meaningful conflict-of-interest rules.

Whether these provisions shall ultimately be absolutely unamendable or subject to a Refounding Process remains to be determined.

# ARTICLE XXVII
## Interpretation

### Section 27.1 — Harmonious Interpretation

Provisions should be interpreted, where reasonably possible, to give effect to the Constitution as a coherent whole.

### Section 27.2 — Purposive Interpretation

Ambiguity shall be interpreted in light of:

- the Preamble;
- the Foundational Constitutional Principles;
- the purpose of the provision;
- the constitutional distribution of authority;
- and the protection against concentration and abuse of power.

### Section 27.3 — Specific Over General

Where provisions cannot reasonably be harmonized, a specific provision ordinarily governs a more general provision concerning the same subject.

### Section 27.4 — Higher Over Lower

A subordinate governing instrument may not override this Constitution.

### Section 27.5 — Later Amendment

A valid later constitutional amendment governs an inconsistent earlier constitutional provision to the extent intended by the amendment, subject to Entrenched provisions.

### Section 27.6 — Religious Questions

Questions requiring substantive doctrinal judgment shall be referred to the competent ecclesiastical authority rather than resolved solely through secular governance reasoning.

### Section 27.7 — Constitutional Questions

Questions concerning:

- jurisdiction;
- validity;
- hierarchy of governing instruments;
- amendment;
- constitutional procedure;
- or structural authority

shall fall within constitutional interpretive jurisdiction.

# ARTICLE XXVIII
## Constitutional Anti-Capture Doctrine

### Section 28.1 — Concentration Risk

The institutional design shall seek to prevent control over all major forms of power from becoming concentrated permanently in one person or faction.

### Section 28.2 — Major Powers

Major constitutional powers include:

- doctrine;
- leadership selection;
- succession;
- discipline;
- adjudication;
- constitutional interpretation;
- money;
- property;
- membership control;
- information;
- amendment.

### Section 28.3 — Incompatible Functions

Subordinate law shall identify offices that may not ordinarily be consolidated.

The mature order should presumptively avoid one individual simultaneously serving as:

- Presiding Spiritual Leader;
- chair of the civil Board;
- chief financial officer or treasurer;
- principal disciplinary adjudicator;
- and constitutional guardian.

### Section 28.4 — Small-Organization Exception

During early formation, practical necessity may require temporary overlapping responsibilities.

Any such overlap shall be:

- disclosed;
- limited;
- reviewed;
- and eliminated when reasonably practicable.

# ARTICLE XXIX
## Founding-to-Mature Transition

### Section 29.1 — Constitutional Objective

The founding generation shall intentionally build institutions capable of surviving it.

### Section 29.2 — Three Constitutional Eras

The governance order may recognize:

#### Era I — Founding Period

The initial vision is articulated and institutions are established.

#### Era II — Transitional Institutionalization

Special founding powers diminish while durable institutions assume their permanent functions.

#### Era III — Mature Constitutional Order

Governance no longer depends upon the continuing authority of the Founder or first generation.

### Section 29.3 — Transition Shall Not Depend Solely on Founder Discretion

The beginning and end of each era shall be determined by objective constitutional rules or events rather than solely by the wishes of the incumbent Founder.

### Section 29.4 — Mature-State Test

The religious order shall be considered constitutionally mature only when it can continue coherently after the departure of every original founder and first-generation officeholder.

# ARTICLE XXX
## Constitutional Decision Classes

To ensure that procedural safeguards correspond to significance, subordinate law shall classify governance acts at least substantially as follows.

### Class A — Ordinary

Routine administration and ordinary religious operations.

### Class B — Significant

Actions with substantial operational, personnel, religious, or financial consequences.

### Class C — Major

Actions involving senior leadership, major property, significant institutional restructuring, or other high-impact matters.

### Class D — Constitutional

Actions altering constitutional structures, senior ecclesiastical authority, major doctrine, affiliation, or comparable constitutional interests.

### Class E — Foundational

Actions potentially altering the essential religious identity, existence, or fundamental constitutional commitments of the religious order.

Procedural burdens, notice, deliberation, voting thresholds, concurrence, and review shall increase with the decision class.

# ARTICLE XXXIV
## Constitutional Standard of Governance

Every office, council, tribunal, board interface, and governing institution established beneath this Constitution shall be evaluated by asking:

1. What legitimate purpose does this authority serve?
2. Why must this authority exist?
3. Who possesses it?
4. By what source is it granted?
5. What qualifications are required?
6. What is its jurisdiction?
7. What is outside its jurisdiction?
8. What rules constrain it?
9. What process must it follow?
10. What constitutes a valid act?
11. What conflicts require recusal?
12. How are decisions recorded?
13. Who reviews errors?
14. Who hears appeals?
15. Who investigates misconduct?
16. Who may remove the officeholder?
17. What occurs upon incapacity?
18. What occurs upon vacancy?
19. How is succession accomplished?
20. What emergency authority exists?
21. How can the institution itself be amended?
22. What prevents capture of the institution?
23. What protects persons affected by its power?
24. How does the authority return to constitutional limits after an emergency?
25. Can the system operate lawfully if the current officeholder is unwise, conflicted, incapacitated, or hostile to institutional restraints?

No institution should receive substantial authority unless these questions can be answered adequately.

# ARTICLE XXXV
## Ultimate Constitutional Test

The validity of this constitutional order shall not be judged solely by how well it functions when wise, benevolent, competent, and mutually trusting people occupy every office.

A durable religious constitution must also function when:

- leaders disagree;
- a leader becomes incapacitated;
- a leader becomes abusive;
- a leader becomes incompetent;
- councils divide;
- factions form;
- money becomes scarce;
- donations become abundant;
- doctrine is disputed;
- a founder leaves;
- a founder dies;
- a successor lacks the founder's charisma;
- communities expand geographically;
- institutions become bureaucratic;
- misconduct is alleged;
- reputation is threatened;
- and people exercising power are tempted to preserve that power.

Accordingly, the constitutional objective is:

> **To preserve the religious mission without making the mission dependent upon the virtue of any single human officeholder.**

And:

> **To create institutions strong enough to preserve continuity, yet constrained enough to permit correction.**

And:

> **To create community participation meaningful enough to confer responsibility, yet structured enough to preserve competent governance.**

And:

> **To create religious authority strong enough to lead, teach, and preserve identity, yet accountable enough that spiritual authority cannot become personal domination.**
