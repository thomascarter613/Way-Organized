# Revelation versus Divination — Historical Boundary Analysis

## Problem
Later Abrahamic traditions often distinguish legitimate “revelation/prophecy” sharply from prohibited “divination.” Historically, the ancient world presents a continuum of claimed information channels.

## Continuum
1. **Unsolicited signs** — celestial, terrestrial, animal, birth anomalies
2. **Solicited technical signs** — extispicy, lots, oil, oracle bones
3. **Dreams** — spontaneous but interpreted through learned systems
4. **Oracles** — a question is deliberately posed to a deity
5. **Possession/ecstatic speech** — deity believed to speak through intermediary
6. **Messenger prophecy** — spontaneous or commissioned speech attributed to deity
7. **Visionary commission** — image/voice plus mission
8. **Scriptural revelation** — extended communication forming sacred text
9. **Illumination/awakening** — direct knowledge without a speaking agent

## Historical observation
The boundary between categories 2–7 is socially/theologically negotiated. Ancient societies could trust one channel, distrust another, or rank them differently. Even within one society dreams could be both suspect and authoritative.

## HTERP rule
Do not infer a causal difference merely because a later tradition labels one practice “revelation” and another “divination.” Compare event structure first:
- solicitation?
- sensory modality?
- specialist technique?
- agent identity?
- propositional content?
- prediction?
- social authority?
- reproducibility?

## Theological relevance
A later religion may still have principled reasons to permit one practice and reject another. HTERP records those theological reasons separately from historical phenomenology.
