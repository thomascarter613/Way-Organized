# Divine-Name and Deity Genealogy — El, Elyon, Shaddai, YHWH

## Scope
Historical-religions reconstruction, not a theological declaration about whether the names ultimately refer to one transcendent reality.

## 1. El
El is both a Semitic word for “god” and the name/title of the senior deity in West-Semitic religion. Theodore J. Lewis surveys El as father, divine kinsman, benevolent protector, and head of a divine council, and explores El traditions such as El Shaddai, El Elyon, El Roi, El Olam, El Bethel, and El Berith.

Patriarchal names and traditions preserve unusually dense El-language. This supports the historical proposition that ancestral Israelite religion incorporated old El traditions.

## 2. Elyon
*Elyon* means “Most High.” Whether El and Elyon were always one deity/title or could be distinguished in some early contexts remains debated. HTERP therefore treats “El Elyon” as a historical designation whose precise pre-Israelite ontology cannot be assumed from later monotheistic usage.

## 3. El Shaddai
El Shaddai belongs to the patriarchal divine-name complex; the exact etymology of *Shaddai* remains uncertain. Exodus 6:2–3 is literarily important because it itself distinguishes patriarchal appearance as El Shaddai from revelation of the name YHWH to Moses, even though other Pentateuchal layers use YHWH in patriarchal narratives.

## 4. YHWH
YHWH has a distinct extra-biblical and poetic trail. Lewis reviews:
- Egyptian geographical lists of the 14th–13th centuries BCE relevant to YHW/Shasu discussion;
- later epigraphic Yahwistic evidence;
- archaic Hebrew poetry associating YHWH's theophany with southern wilderness geography;
- Midianite-Kenite and related southern-origin hypotheses.

The exact homeland and original character of YHWH remain debated. “Southern association” is stronger than any single pinpoint origin claim.

## 5. El–YHWH identification
The most economical historical model is not that one name instantly replaced another. Instead, Israelite religion increasingly identifies YHWH with El and transfers/merges El's high-god functions and titles into Yahwistic theology.

This model helps explain:
- El elements in ancestral traditions and the name Israel;
- YHWH's eventual role as creator, father, king, judge, and head of the divine council;
- texts that can preserve older divine-council structures while later theology asserts exclusive monotheism.

## 6. Deuteronomy 32:8–9
The older textual form is widely discussed as reading “sons of God/divine beings” rather than the Masoretic “sons of Israel.” In that form, Elyon allocates nations among divine beings and YHWH receives Jacob/Israel as his portion.

Interpretations differ:
1. Elyon/El and YHWH are distinct in an older layer;
2. Elyon is already a title of YHWH, who allocates nations to subordinate beings.

HTERP records the passage as important evidence for divine-council theology but does not treat one interpretation as settled merely because it fits an evolutionary model.

## 7. Kuntillet Ajrud and “his asherah”
Eighth-century BCE inscriptions invoke “YHWH of Samaria” and “YHWH of Teman” with *asherah*. Scholarship disputes whether this refers to the goddess Asherah, a cultic symbol/object, sanctuary, or another sacred referent. The inscriptions nevertheless demonstrate localized Yahwistic formulations and a religious world less tidy than later exclusive-monotheist presentation.

## 8. HTERP theological firewall
Historical reconstruction permits:
- previously distinct cultic profiles becoming identified;
- titles moving between traditions;
- progressive monolatry/monotheism;
- theological reinterpretation of inherited categories.

It does **not** decide whether:
- humans gradually invented one God out of many;
- one real transcendent intelligence progressively disclosed itself through changing names;
- multiple intelligences were involved;
- some combination occurred.

Those are downstream H3/H4/H5 questions.
