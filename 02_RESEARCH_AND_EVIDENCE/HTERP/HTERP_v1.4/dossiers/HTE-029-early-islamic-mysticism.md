# HTE-029 — Early Islamic Asceticism and Sufi Mysticism

## Scope
The movement from early Muslim **zuhd** (ascetic piety) toward identifiable Sufi practices and theories.

## Historical structure
David Cook describes early Sufism as developing from Qur'anic ascetic/pietistic tendencies interacting with a wider late-antique ascetic world. By roughly the tenth century, Sufism had become a distinctive Islamic mystical tradition.

Hasan al-Basri (642–728) is securely an important early preacher, jurist, Qur'an reciter, and ascetic. Encyclopaedia Iranica stresses that he was not called a "Sufi" in his own lifetime and that later factions claimed him retrospectively.

Rabi'a al-Adawiyya became a paradigmatic figure of divine love, but many famous stories and sayings survive in later hagiographical layers and require attribution control.

## Practice taxonomy
Later and developed Sufi practice includes:
- **dhikr** — remembrance/repetition of divine names or Qur'anic phrases;
- **muraqaba** — contemplative attention to God's presence;
- **sama'** — listening/music in some traditions;
- litanies, prayer, fasting, vigil, ethical self-examination.

These practices cultivate states; they are not simply unsolicited messages.

## Phenomenology
Reported Sufi experience may include:
- presence/nearness;
- love/longing;
- annihilation of ego-language (*fana*) in later theory;
- illumination/knowledge;
- visions/dreams;
- intense affect during remembrance.

## HTERP caution
Later mature Sufi metaphysics must not be retrojected into seventh/eighth-century asceticism.

## Comparison value
Sufism demonstrates how a revealed/scriptural religion can develop a **cultivated interior path** while grounding it in earlier prophetic revelation.
