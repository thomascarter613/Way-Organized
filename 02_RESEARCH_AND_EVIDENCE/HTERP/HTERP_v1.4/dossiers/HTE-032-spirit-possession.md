# HTE-032 — Spirit Possession as Intermediary Phenomenon

## Scope
A comparative possession dossier, with African and Afro-Atlantic ethnographic material used as controls. It does **not** assume a single “African possession religion.”

## Why possession needs its own class
Cross-cultural scholarship distinguishes at least:
- **executive possession:** another identity/agent is understood to take control;
- **pathogenic possession:** an intrusive spirit is understood to cause illness/misfortune.

Other traditions include mediumistic possession in which a spirit speaks through a specialist.

## Observable features
Depending on tradition:
- altered voice/posture;
- identity shift;
- amnesia or partial memory;
- culturally stereotyped behaviors;
- ritual induction through music/dance;
- healing/diagnosis;
- pronouncements attributed to a spirit;
- social authority of medium/diviner.

## Evidence
Anthropological observation establishes that possession practices and possession interpretations are widespread and socially consequential. It does not independently establish that a distinct discarnate agent controls the body.

## Cognitive/social controls
Emma Cohen's work demonstrates that possession can be analyzed through cultural transmission, social cognition, agency representation, and neuroscience without dismissing participants' own cultural meanings.

## HTERP test
The most discriminating possession cases would involve:
- verifiable information inaccessible to the possessed person;
- stable agent-specific knowledge across separated mediums;
- preregistered, blinded conditions;
- physiological/behavioral patterns beyond trained role enactment.

## Cultural ethics
Case studies must be tradition-specific; “possession” is an analyst's comparative category, not necessarily a community's own ontology.

## Baseline sources
- Emma Cohen, *The Mind Possessed* (OUP).
- Emma Cohen, “What is Spirit Possession?” *Ethnos* (2008).
- Robert Torrance, “The Varieties of Spirit Possession.”
