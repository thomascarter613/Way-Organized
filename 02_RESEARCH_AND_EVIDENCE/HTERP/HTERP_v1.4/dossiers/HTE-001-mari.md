# HTE-001 — Prophetic Communication at Mari

## Identification
- **Setting:** Kingdom of Mari and associated Syrian-Mesopotamian polities
- **Principal period:** chiefly eighteenth century BCE, especially the reign of Zimri-Lim
- **Surviving language:** Akkadian cuneiform
- **Encounter classes:** MSG; possible AUD/VIS/DRM/POS depending on report
- **Primary historical value:** contemporary or near-contemporary evidence for an actual prophetic institution

## What the evidence establishes

The Mari archives contain legal, administrative, ritual, and especially epistolary evidence for persons who delivered communications attributed to deities. Martti Nissinen's synthesis emphasizes that letters report prophetic performances to kings and that written oracles can preserve early transcripts of spoken oracles. Mari is one of the two largest surviving ancient Near Eastern prophetic corpora, the other being Neo-Assyrian prophecy.

Prophets appear in temple settings and political communication. The surviving evidence includes both male and female intermediaries. Technical titles vary; modern translations such as “prophet,” “answerer,” and “ecstatic” simplify ancient categories and must not be assumed to designate one uniform subjective state.

### Directly established
- people recognized as prophetic intermediaries existed as social actors;
- officials and court figures transmitted their messages;
- messages were attributed to named deities;
- prophecy interacted with temples, kingship, war, cult, and political decision-making.

### Not directly established
- what every intermediary subjectively experienced;
- whether the message originated in an externally existing deity;
- whether a given oracle contained genuinely inaccessible information.

## Source assessment
- **Practice/institution:** P2, very strong
- **Specific reported oracle:** commonly P2
- **First-person phenomenology:** usually weak
- **Metaphysical interpretation:** unverified

## Phenomenology

The evidence is much stronger for **performance** than for introspection. Some categories are associated by scholars with ecstatic or liminal behavior, but the archive cannot justify treating every prophetic message as trance, possession, or auditory hallucination.

This is a crucial HTERP calibration point: a society can leave excellent evidence that “prophecy happened” while leaving poor evidence for what was happening in the prophet's consciousness.

## Content profile
Typical concerns include:
- royal legitimacy,
- warfare and security,
- offerings and cultic obligations,
- divine support or warning,
- political and dynastic affairs.

Moral content exists but is not uniformly the central feature in the later Israelite sense of broad social-ethical critique.

## Dependency position
Mari belongs to the wider ancient Near Eastern divinatory-prophetic ecosystem. Similarities with biblical prophecy should therefore initially be treated as **historically connected or structurally inherited possibilities**, not as independent confirmation of supernatural contact.

## Hypothesis analysis

### H0 — constructed/political
Plausible for some individual messages: transmitting political or cultic interests under divine authority could serve human institutions. No warrant exists to apply H0 universally.

### H1 — cognitive
Dream, ecstatic, voice-hearing, or dissociative mechanisms are possible for some cases, but the surviving evidence is usually too thin for retrospective diagnosis.

### H2 — cultural
Strongly supported as a shaping mechanism. Deity identity, temple context, royal interests, and message form are embedded in Mari culture.

### H3/H4 — transcendent
Compatible with how contemporaries interpreted the events, but the documents do not independently discriminate transcendent from human causes.

### H5 — mixed
Best working stance.

## Discriminating evidence sought
1. securely dated oracle before an outcome;
2. specific prediction with narrow failure conditions;
3. evidence that relevant information was unavailable through ordinary political channels;
4. independent reporting of the outcome;
5. evidence against post-event rewriting.
