# HTE-025 — Earliest Christian Prophecy

## Scope
This dossier treats early Christian prophecy as a family of practices rather than one uniform phenomenon.

## Principal witnesses
- Paul's first letter to the Corinthians: prophecy as a charismatic gift within communal worship.
- Revelation/Apocalypse of John: extended visionary prophecy.
- Didache: traveling/local prophets embedded in community discipline, with concern about false prophets.
- Shepherd of Hermas: dreams/visions and angelic revelation.
- late second-century New Prophecy/Montanism: ecstatic/eschatological prophecy associated especially with Montanus, Prisca, and Maximilla.

David Aune notes that the major literary witnesses diverge enough in time, geography, and ideology that a single synthesis of "the Christian prophet" is difficult.

## Source profile

### Paul
First-person/near-contemporary evidence that prophecy operated in first-century assemblies. In 1 Corinthians, prophecy is treated as a community gift and is subject to discernment.

### Didache
Likely late first to early second century in its received form, itself a redacted composite. It regulates apostles, prophets, teachers, hospitality, and criteria for problematic prophets.

### Shepherd of Hermas
A second-century Roman apocalypse containing multiple visions, commandments, and parables mediated by revelatory figures.

### Montanism / New Prophecy
Late second-century prophetic movement in Phrygia. Surviving evidence is heavily mediated through supporters and opponents, so heresiological framing must be controlled.

## Phenomenology
The corpus includes:
- inspired speech;
- visions;
- dreams;
- angelic mediators;
- eschatological messages;
- communal discernment;
- in some later accounts, ecstatic states.

## HTERP result
Christian prophecy did not simply cease with Jesus or the apostles in the earliest communities. The historical shift is better represented as:
**charismatic prophecy → community testing/regulation → competition with emerging offices/canon → continued visionary and dissident forms**.

That does not mean bishops simply "suppressed prophecy"; the evidence shows multiple mechanisms of institutionalization and boundary-making.
