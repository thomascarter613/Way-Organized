# Torah Slavery Laws

## Source cluster
Exodus 21; Deuteronomy 15; Leviticus 25; related narratives/laws.

## Historical finding
Slavery/servitude was an accepted institution throughout the ancient Near East. Biblical law regulates several forms rather than abolishing the institution.

Scholarship distinguishes:
- debt-servitude/debt-slavery;
- war/capture slavery;
- foreign chattel slavery;
- sale of family members under economic pressure.

Exodus 21's opening laws concern debt-servitude and female slave-wife arrangements. Leviticus 25 distinguishes Israelite debt service from the acquisition of foreign slaves as inheritable property.

## Strongest charitable reading
Compared with some surrounding systems, particular biblical laws can:
- limit debt service;
- require release;
- penalize kidnapping;
- give certain protections;
- tie liberation ethics to memory of Egypt.

These restraints matter historically.

## Strongest ethical objection
Regulation is not abolition.

The Hebrew Bible:
- permits ownership of human beings;
- preserves unequal status by ethnicity/status;
- permits coercive labor/property relations;
- includes gendered vulnerabilities.

Calling all biblical slavery merely “employment” is historically inaccurate.

## Later reception
Biblical slavery texts were used both:
- to defend slaveholding;
- and by abolitionists emphasizing Exodus, human dignity, and liberation.

## Constructive decision
**REJECT NORMATIVE FORCE of human ownership.**

The Provisional Way holds:
> No human being may be owned as property.

Retain:
- liberation memory;
- debt relief;
- release;
- anti-kidnapping;
- humane-treatment trajectories.

Reject:
- slave ownership;
- inherited human property;
- coerced slave marriage;
- ethnicity-based permanent servitude.

## Constitutional reason
Human dignity and anti-exploitation protections outrank ancient property structures.
