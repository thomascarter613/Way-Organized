Would it be weird, odd, etc. if someone wanted to create a novel religion that combined judaism, christianity, islam, and maybe some other streams

No. It would be **unusual**, but not inherently weird. Religions have repeatedly emerged through reinterpretation, synthesis, reform, restoration, and contact between existing traditions. What would matter much more is whether the result formed a **coherent religious system** rather than simply collecting attractive pieces from several religions.

Judaism, Christianity, and Islam are actually unusually interesting candidates for such a project because they already share a substantial conceptual ancestry: Abrahamic monotheism, creation, prophecy, revelation, moral accountability, prayer, fasting, charity, sacred history, angels, judgment, and many overlapping figures such as Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses, David, and others. Christianity and Islam additionally have major claims about Jesus, although they interpret him very differently.

The difficult part is that their disagreements aren't merely cosmetic. They make mutually incompatible claims about some foundational subjects. For example, Christianity traditionally affirms Jesus' divinity, crucifixion, resurrection, and usually the Trinity; Islam explicitly rejects the divinity of Jesus and the Trinity and understands Muhammad as the final prophet; Judaism rejects both Christian claims about Jesus and Islamic claims about Muhammad as developments outside Judaism. There are similarly major disagreements regarding Torah, covenant, revelation, salvation, religious law, scripture, religious authority, and the nature of God.

So there are several intellectually different things someone might create:

* **An Abrahamic synthesis** — Judaism, Christianity, and Islam are partial historical expressions of one continuing revelation, and the new religion attempts to reconcile them.
* **A restorationist religion** — argues that there was originally one Abrahamic faith that became fragmented, and seeks to reconstruct it.
* **A perennialist religion** — regards the three religions, and possibly others, as culturally conditioned expressions of a deeper transcendent reality.
* **A new revelation religion** — accepts earlier traditions but claims that additional revelation resolves their disagreements.
* **A rational reconstruction** — accepts certain historical, ethical, mystical, and ritual elements while rejecting claims that cannot be reconciled.
* **An interfaith spiritual tradition** — does not claim the older religions are wrong, but constructs a shared practice from compatible elements.

The distinction is important. Saying, for example, "Christians, Muslims, and Jews all ultimately worship the same God, and we should learn from all three" is comparatively easy. Saying "the Qur'an is correct that Jesus is not divine while the Nicene Creed is also literally correct that Jesus is God" produces an actual contradiction. A serious synthetic religion therefore needs **rules for resolving contradictions**.

That might be the central intellectual problem of the whole undertaking.

You would need to answer something like:

> When two inherited traditions make incompatible claims, how do we determine what is true?

Possible answers include historical criticism, reason, revelation, mystical experience, ethical consequences, prophetic authority, consensus, scripture hierarchy, or some combination of these.

And once you answer that question, you are no longer merely mixing religions. You have created an **epistemology of religion**—a theory explaining how human beings know religious truth.

That is where such a project could become genuinely interesting.

There is another important distinction between **drawing from traditions** and claiming to represent them. A new religion could legitimately say, "We derive this practice from Jewish tradition, this theological concept from Islam, and this teaching from Christianity." It would be much more controversial to say, "This is what Judaism/Christianity/Islam really teaches," particularly when adherents of those religions explicitly disagree. Intellectual honesty about sources would make an enormous difference.

And adding traditions beyond the Abrahamic three could actually make the project richer rather than stranger. For example, one could investigate Buddhist approaches to suffering and mental discipline, Hindu philosophical treatments of ultimate reality, Zoroastrian moral cosmology, Taoist ideas about natural order, Stoic ethics, Indigenous traditions concerning community and land, Greek philosophy, or ancient Near Eastern religious concepts. But at that point you would need to decide whether the religion is fundamentally **Abrahamic with outside influences** or genuinely **universalist**.

There are also precedents for religions developing in religiously plural environments. Sikhism emerged in a setting shaped by Hindu and Islamic traditions, although Sikhs rightly understand Sikhism as its own revealed tradition rather than simply a Hindu-Islamic mixture. The Baháʼí Faith recognizes figures from several previous religions within a framework of progressive revelation. Unitarian Universalism developed into an explicitly pluralistic religious tradition. Various smaller movements have attempted Christian-Muslim or wider Abrahamic synthesis as well. So the general phenomenon is not historically unprecedented.

What would make a proposed religion seem unserious isn't its novelty. It would be things such as indiscriminately combining contradictory doctrines, inventing dubious historical claims to provide legitimacy, appropriating rituals without understanding them, or constructing the religion entirely around the founder's personal authority.

Conversely, a surprisingly rigorous religion could be constructed by starting from first principles:

**Metaphysics → theology → revelation → anthropology → ethics → religious law → spiritual practice → ritual → community → governance → scripture/canon → interpretation → eschatology.**

Then, for every question, you could compare what Judaism, Christianity, Islam, and other traditions say and make an explicit determination:

**retain / reinterpret / synthesize / reject / leave unresolved.**

That would be a very different exercise from merely inventing ceremonies or picking favorite doctrines. It could become a serious comparative-theological project.

Given the direction of your question, I think an especially interesting way to approach it would be to **design the religion systematically from zero**, without initially deciding what the answers should be. We could construct perhaps 100–200 foundational questions—starting with *Does God exist? What is God? What counts as revelation?* and eventually reaching things like prayer, dietary rules, clergy, marriage, holidays, governance, death, and succession—and then compare the Jewish, Christian, Islamic, philosophical, and other possible answers before constructing the synthesis.



I find that there are differences between what one might term the "original teachings" of a prophet/teacher and the religion that formed after them and claiming to follow them.

Yes. That distinction is historically important, and it may give you a much stronger foundation for the kind of project you are imagining.

The key is to separate at least three things that often get collapsed together: **the historical teacher or prophetic movement**, **the earliest community interpreting that figure**, and **the later institutional religion** that develops over generations or centuries.

Those three are rarely identical.

With Jesus, for example, there is a difference between asking, “What did Jesus most likely teach in first-century Judea?” and asking, “What does later Christianity teach?” Christianity contains centuries of theological development about Christology, Trinity, sacraments, ecclesiology, canon, original sin, atonement, clerical authority, and other matters. Some Christians understand those developments as legitimate unfoldings of what was already implicit in Jesus and the apostles; others—including many historians and restorationist movements—see significant discontinuities.

Something similar can be said about Islam, although the historical situation is different. You can distinguish Muhammad's preaching and the Qur'anic movement from the later development of hadith collections, jurisprudential schools, systematic theology, caliphal institutions, Sufi traditions, sectarian divisions, and detailed legal doctrine. Muslims disagree internally about how much of that later tradition is authoritative and how directly it represents Muhammad.

Judaism is more complicated because there is not really one historical founder whose original teaching can simply be recovered. You have layers: traditions associated with the patriarchs, Moses and Torah, prophets, Second Temple Judaism, Pharisaic traditions, the Mishnah, Talmud, medieval halakhic development, philosophy, Kabbalah, and later movements. Rabbinic Judaism is historically continuous with ancient Judaism but also represents enormous post-Temple development.

So a useful model might be:

**Founder/revelation → original movement → first-generation interpretation → canon formation → institutionalization → doctrinal development → legal/ritual elaboration → political entanglement → later traditions.**

And that creates a very interesting possibility for your proposed religion.

Rather than saying:

> “I want to combine Judaism, Christianity, and Islam.”

you could formulate the project more precisely as:

> “I want to investigate the earliest recoverable Abrahamic religious teachings and determine whether a coherent common tradition can be reconstructed beneath later institutional divergence.”

That is a much more intellectually substantial undertaking.

But there is one major methodological danger: the phrase **“original teaching” can easily become whatever the researcher personally prefers**. So you would need a disciplined method for determining what counts as early or authentic.

I would distinguish at least five evidentiary categories:

1. **Direct or near-direct textual evidence** — material reasonably close in time to the teacher or movement.
2. **Early independent attestation** — teachings appearing in multiple relatively independent sources.
3. **Historical context** — whether a teaching makes sense within the culture, language, politics, and religion of the teacher's actual period.
4. **Later doctrinal development** — beliefs that clearly become articulated substantially later.
5. **Uncertainty** — cases where we simply cannot responsibly determine what the original position was.

That last category is crucial. A serious reconstruction would sometimes have to say, **“unknown.”**

You could then ask a very provocative historical question across the Abrahamic traditions:

**If Abraham, Moses, the Hebrew prophets, Jesus, and Muhammad were placed in one room, what religious propositions would they actually agree on?**

Probably more than later religious polemics suggest, but considerably less than a simplistic “all religions teach the same thing” approach would imply.

For instance, one might investigate whether an early common core includes things like uncompromising devotion to one God, rejection of idolatry, repentance, justice, mercy, care for the poor, prayer, fasting, charity, moral accountability, judgment, humility before God, and the prophetic obligation to confront corruption. Those propositions can then be tested historically rather than simply assumed.

Then the most interesting questions are the points where the traditions diverge:

**Who or what is Jesus?**
**Did Jesus intend to establish a new religion?**
**Was Torah intended to remain binding?**
**What did Muhammad understand himself to be restoring?**
**What constitutes revelation?**
**Can prophecy continue?**
**Does God require a priesthood?**
**What makes religious law legitimate?**
**Did these teachers intend centralized religious institutions at all?**

That approach also creates an important distinction between **restoration** and **syncretism**.

Syncretism says, roughly, “take elements from A, B, and C and combine them.”

Restoration says, “A, B, and C may contain fragments, developments, and interpretations of something older; let's attempt to recover the underlying thing.”

Your idea sounds increasingly like the second.

And if you pursued it rigorously, I would not start by designing doctrines or rituals. I would start by creating a **historical reconstruction layer** for each major figure or movement:

**Abrahamic tradition → Moses/Torah → Hebrew prophets → Second Temple Judaism → Jesus movement → earliest Christianity → Qur'anic/Muhammadan movement → earliest Islam.**

For each layer we could separate:

**high-confidence original material / plausible material / later development / disputed material / clearly late material.**

Only after doing that would we attempt synthesis.

That would prevent us from accidentally constructing a new religion out of later denominational assumptions while believing we were returning to something ancient.

And there is an even deeper question lurking underneath all of this: perhaps the unit of analysis should not actually be **Judaism + Christianity + Islam** at all. It might instead be the much longer historical phenomenon we could provisionally call the **Abrahamic prophetic tradition**, with Judaism, Christianity, and Islam treated as major later branches of it.

That framing could substantially change the project.

Yes. That is a much more coherent concept than simply “combining Judaism, Christianity, and Islam.”

What you are describing sounds more like a **restorationist prophetic tradition with concentric layers of authority**. The center would not be the later religions as institutions. It would be the earliest recoverable teachings associated with the major prophetic streams, with later material admitted according to how well it accords with that core.

A workable structure might look something like this:

1. **Foundational layer — highest authority**

   * Torah, though interpreted with attention to historical setting, genre, textual development, and the distinction between enduring principles and historically situated legislation.
   * The Hebrew prophets.
   * The most historically defensible teachings of Jesus.
   * The Qur'an and the most historically defensible teachings of Muhammad.

2. **Early witness layer — important but subordinate**

   * Psalms and wisdom literature.
   * Early Jewish literature.
   * Earliest Jesus-movement writings.
   * Early Christian writings outside sayings directly attributable to Jesus.
   * Early Islamic reports and traditions.
   * Material illuminating how the original communities understood their teachers.

3. **Interpretive tradition — useful but nonbinding**

   * Rabbinic commentary.
   * Church Fathers and later Christian theologians.
   * Hadith, tafsir, fiqh, and Islamic theology.
   * Jewish, Christian, and Islamic mysticism.
   * Philosophical traditions.

4. **Consonant wisdom — optional**
   Material from outside the Abrahamic traditions that agrees with the foundational principles rather than contradicting them. This could include selected insights from Stoicism, Buddhism, Taoism, Hindu philosophy, Zoroastrianism, Sikhism, Indigenous traditions, Greek philosophy, or other sources.

That last category could be quite powerful if handled carefully. It means something does not become true merely because it came from an Abrahamic source, nor false merely because it did not.

The governing principle might instead be:

> Truth is received wherever it is found, but every teaching is tested against the foundational revelation, reason, moral coherence, historical evidence, and its fruits in human life.

That immediately gives the religion a way to interact with material outside its core without endlessly expanding the canon.

The distinction between **required** and **permitted** teachings also solves a major problem that religions frequently encounter.

You could have something like:

**Core doctrine** — expected of everyone.

**Authorized interpretation** — legitimate conclusions people may hold but which are not mandatory.

**Permitted speculation** — questions on which the community explicitly allows disagreement.

**Wisdom tradition** — teachings and practices worth studying but carrying no revelatory authority.

**Rejected teaching** — incompatible with the foundational principles.

That would allow considerable intellectual diversity without making the religion doctrinally empty.

### The difficult issue: “actual teachings”

This is probably where most of the serious work would occur.

For Moses, Jesus, and Muhammad, the evidence is not identical.

For Jesus, for example, we don't possess writings authored by Jesus himself. We have traditions about his teachings preserved primarily in early Christian writings. So instead of claiming to possess *the actual words of Jesus* in every instance, the system should probably use epistemic categories such as:

**Very high confidence**
**High confidence**
**Plausible**
**Uncertain**
**Probably later interpretation**

The same principle could be applied wherever textual history makes certainty impossible.

That would actually give your religion a feature that many traditional systems don't formally possess: **doctrinal confidence levels**.

Instead of pretending that every conclusion has equal certainty, it could explicitly say:

> We consider proposition X foundational.

versus

> We consider proposition Y likely but historically uncertain.

versus

> The tradition permits multiple interpretations of proposition Z.

That is potentially an extremely important innovation.

### It would also help to distinguish revelation from commentary

Suppose you adopted the Torah, prophets, Jesus, and Muhammad as the central stream.

You could establish something like an **authority hierarchy**:

**God → revelation → prophetic teaching → earliest reliable witness → reasoned interpretation → accumulated tradition → individual opinion.**

Nothing lower in the hierarchy could overturn something demonstrably higher without exceptionally strong justification.

That would prevent the common phenomenon where centuries of commentary effectively become more authoritative in practice than the text they supposedly interpret.

It would also allow you to say something nuanced about later Judaism, Christianity, and Islam:

They would not necessarily be rejected.

Instead, they become enormous repositories of **interpretation, scholarship, spiritual practice, legal reasoning, historical experience, and wisdom**.

You could mine all three extensively without treating every later doctrine as binding.

### There is another especially interesting possibility

The religion doesn't necessarily have to say:

**Moses → Judaism → Jesus → Christianity → Muhammad → Islam.**

It could understand the history as:

**One God → recurring prophetic revelation → recurring human communities → repeated institutionalization and reinterpretation.**

In that model, “Judaism,” “Christianity,” and “Islam” are historical manifestations surrounding particular moments within a longer prophetic history.

The actual religion would therefore not be a merger of three religions.

It might claim to be an attempt to recover the **continuous prophetic way** underlying them.

Conceptually, something like:

**Abraham → Moses → Prophets → Jesus → Muhammad → continuing faithful community**

rather than three separate boxes.

And that raises an important question concerning Muhammad.

You would eventually need to decide whether Muhammad is understood as:

**the final prophet**,
**the final major legislative prophet**,
**the latest historically authenticated prophet**,
or simply **one prophet within an indefinitely continuing prophetic tradition**.

Likewise with Jesus, the system eventually has to decide whether he is:

**prophet**,
**Messiah**,
**uniquely anointed teacher**,
**Son of God in some defined sense**,
or something else.

Those questions cannot permanently be avoided because they affect the underlying theology.

But they **can be postponed** while the historical investigation is undertaken.

### And I think optional layers are exactly the right instinct

You could visualize the religion as concentric rings:

**Center:** God and foundational prophetic revelation.

**Ring 1:** Torah + Prophets + Jesus + Qur'an/Muhammad.

**Ring 2:** earliest communities and explanatory traditions.

**Ring 3:** Jewish, Christian, and Islamic theological and mystical heritage.

**Ring 4:** compatible wisdom from the world's religious and philosophical traditions.

**Ring 5:** modern scholarship, science, philosophy, ethics, and continuing human inquiry.

The farther outward something is, the less authority it possesses—but it can still be valuable.

Importantly, **older would not automatically mean more authoritative** either. A very ancient idea could simply be wrong. Age is evidence about historical provenance, not truth.

That distinction would keep the system from degenerating into antiquarianism.

The deeper principle might therefore be:

> **Preserve what is revealed. Recover what is original. Test what is inherited. Accept what is true. Permit uncertainty where knowledge is unavailable.**

That is already close to a theological methodology rather than merely a collection of beliefs.

The logical next thing to construct would be the religion's **epistemological constitution** before writing any creed: exactly what counts as revelation, how textual conflicts are resolved, how historical uncertainty is treated, what role reason plays, how later traditions are evaluated, and what standard allows an outside teaching into the optional wisdom layer. Once those rules exist, we could begin processing Torah, Prophets, Jesus, Muhammad, and the other streams through them systematically.

do all religions have an exoteric and esoteric circle?
￼
---

No. **Not all religions have a formal exoteric/esoteric structure**, but a very large number of religious traditions develop something resembling it.

“Exoteric” and “esoteric” are useful comparative terms, but they can hide important differences between traditions.

An **exoteric** dimension is generally the public religion: beliefs, moral rules, communal worship, festivals, law, scripture, and practices accessible to ordinary members.

An **esoteric** dimension involves teachings or practices that are deeper, initiatory, contemplative, mystical, symbolic, restricted, or intended for people who have undergone particular preparation.

A useful shorthand is:

**Exoteric:** “How should we live and worship?”
**Esoteric:** “What is the deeper reality these teachings and practices point toward?”

But the boundary can take several forms.

In **Judaism**, ordinary Torah observance, prayer, festivals, and halakhah can be distinguished from traditions such as **Merkabah mysticism and Kabbalah**. Historically, some mystical teachings were deliberately restricted to adequately prepared students.

In **Islam**, there is an especially explicit vocabulary for something like this distinction: **ẓāhir**, the outward or apparent dimension, and **bāṭin**, the inward dimension. Islamic law and ordinary religious practice occupy much of the outward sphere, while Sufi traditions emphasize inner purification, remembrance, experiential knowledge of God, and spiritual interpretation. That does not mean all Muslims accept every esoteric interpretation.

Christianity has something similar, although usually less formally organized as two circles. Ordinary Christian doctrine, morality, worship, and sacraments coexist with long traditions of **contemplation and mysticism**: the Desert Fathers, apophatic theology, hesychasm, monastic spirituality, Teresa of Ávila, John of the Cross, Meister Eckhart, and others. Some early Christian writers also distinguished teachings appropriate for beginners from deeper theological contemplation. But mainstream Christianity generally resisted the idea that salvation required possessing a secret doctrine unavailable to ordinary believers—the sort of claim associated with some ancient Gnostic movements.

In **Buddhism**, the distinction can become much more explicit. Many Buddhist teachings are broadly accessible, while **Vajrayāna/Tantric Buddhism** contains practices requiring initiation, transmission, and instruction from a qualified teacher. Some texts and practices traditionally are not supposed to be undertaken without that preparation.

Various **Hindu traditions** have public devotional and ethical forms alongside philosophical, yogic, tantric, or initiatory traditions. Tantra in particular often developed explicit initiatory structures.

Many Indigenous religions also contain distinctions between knowledge available to the whole community and knowledge entrusted only to elders, healers, ritual specialists, or members who have undergone initiation.

But there are also religious movements where this distinction is weak or explicitly rejected. Some forms of Protestant Christianity, for example, strongly emphasize the accessibility of scripture and salvation to everyone and are suspicious of claims that an elite possesses hidden spiritual knowledge. Even there, though, differences in spiritual maturity, theological education, or contemplative experience inevitably arise.

So I would distinguish **four different phenomena** that often get lumped together as “esotericism”:

1. **Secret teaching** — information deliberately withheld from outsiders or novices.
2. **Initiatory teaching** — practices available only after preparation or initiation.
3. **Mystical teaching** — pursuit of experiential knowledge of God or ultimate reality.
4. **Deeper interpretation** — symbolic, allegorical, metaphysical, or contemplative readings underneath the surface meaning of scripture and ritual.

A religion can have any combination of those without necessarily possessing a formal “inner circle.”

For the religion you've been describing, I think this distinction could actually be useful—but I would be cautious about creating a **secret elite possessing doctrines ordinary members aren't allowed to know**. Historically, that can generate enormous problems of authority and abuse.

A more defensible model would be **depth without secrecy**.

The same religion could operate at progressively deeper levels:

**Public way:** ethical conduct, prayer, charity, fasting, community, worship.

**Study:** Torah, Prophets, Jesus, Qur'an, history, language, interpretation.

**Contemplative way:** silence, meditation, remembrance of God, examination of the self, purification of character.

**Mystical/theological inquiry:** questions of divine reality, consciousness, creation, symbolism, union or nearness to God, and deeper scriptural interpretation.

**Advanced disciplines:** practices requiring genuine preparation or supervision, where restriction exists because misuse could be harmful or because prerequisites are necessary—not because an elite is guarding secret truths.

That creates something resembling an exoteric/esoteric structure without saying:

> “The ordinary believers get the fake/simple religion and the insiders learn what we really believe.”

Instead, everyone has access to the **same truth**, but people can penetrate it to different depths.

A particularly elegant principle for the system you're developing might be:

**Nothing necessary for salvation, righteousness, or relationship with God is secret. Deeper understanding may require study, experience, discipline, and maturity.**

That principle would fit surprisingly well with elements of Jewish, Christian, Islamic, and mystical traditions while also protecting against one of the classic dangers of esoteric religious systems.

you mention an Abrahamic prophetic stream, are there other prophetic streams in human history

---

Yes—but we should be careful with the term **“prophetic stream.”** I was using it as a comparative category, not as a standard scholarly label. If we define a prophet narrowly as **someone who receives communication from a transcendent/divine source and transmits it to others**, then prophecy is much wider than the Abrahamic religions. Scholars now treat prophecy as a broader ancient Near Eastern and Mediterranean phenomenon, with documented examples from Mesopotamia, the Levant, Israel, and Greece. ([OUP Academic][1])

The more interesting question for what we're investigating is whether there were **independent streams of claimed revelation**—traditions that might represent separate encounters with whatever transcendent reality lies behind religion.

I would distinguish roughly these:

1. **Ancient Mesopotamian–Levantine prophecy.** This actually predates the biblical prophetic tradition in our surviving records. At Mari in Syria, seventeenth-century BCE tablets record men and women receiving messages attributed to gods and conveying them to rulers. Some were ecstatic figures; others seem to have delivered relatively ordinary verbal revelations. Approximately 65 surviving Mari tablets document this phenomenon. ([Compass Hub][2]) This is particularly significant because biblical prophecy did **not arise in a cultural vacuum**. There was already a recognizable institution of people claiming, essentially, “the deity has communicated this message.”

2. **The Abrahamic/West-Semitic stream.** What we've been discussing—Abraham, Moses, the Hebrew prophets, Jesus, and Muhammad—eventually develops an unusually strong conception of revelation as a continuing historical sequence. Its distinctive feature isn't simply visionary experience; it increasingly presents history itself as a sequence of divine-human encounters, covenant, moral correction, judgment, and renewed revelation.

3. **The Iranian/Zoroastrian stream.** **Zarathustra/Zoroaster** is perhaps the most intriguing non-Abrahamic comparison. The oldest Zoroastrian material portrays him as proclaiming **Ahura Mazda**, “Wise Lord,” and a cosmic moral opposition between *aša*—truth/right order—and the Lie. Scholars commonly regard Zoroaster as an ancient Iranian religious reformer/prophet, although his exact date remains uncertain. ([Iranica Online][3]) Of all the candidates outside the Abrahamic lineage, this one deserves especially serious attention because its structure can look remarkably prophetic: revelation, supreme divine reality, moral choice, judgment, cosmic struggle, and eventual restoration. ([Iranica Online][4])

4. **The Vedic seer tradition.** Ancient Indian religion gives us the **ṛṣis**, literally something closer to “seers” than Abrahamic prophets. Traditional understanding regards the Vedic hymns as *heard* or perceived by inspired seers rather than invented by them. The Rigveda preserves hymns attributed by tradition to particular rishis. ([Wikisource][5]) This represents a somewhat different model: **revelatory perception** rather than “God tells prophet X to tell the people Y.”

5. **Greek and Mediterranean oracle traditions.** Delphi, Didyma, Dodona, and other sanctuaries had people understood to speak under divine inspiration. Modern comparative work finds enough similarities between Greek, Mesopotamian, and biblical prophecy to discuss them as parts of a broader ancient Eastern Mediterranean prophetic phenomenon. ([OUP Academic][6]) The major difference is that an oracle such as Delphi normally wasn't creating a continuing universal revelation or founding a prophetic religion.

6. **South and Central Asian enlightenment/revelatory traditions.** Buddhism and Jainism are somewhat different. The Buddha does not principally present himself as a messenger carrying commands from a creator God; he is an **awakened discoverer of reality**. Jain Tirthankaras similarly reveal or rediscover a path rather than function exactly like biblical prophets. These may therefore belong to another category that could be called an **illumination stream** rather than a prophetic one.

7. **Chinese revelatory traditions.** Early Chinese religion includes divination and communication with ancestors and spirits, while later Daoist movements contain explicit revelations—texts and teachings understood to have been communicated by divine beings. Again, this is genuine revelatory religion, although its cosmology differs substantially from Abrahamic prophecy.

8. **Sikhism.** The Sikh Gurus are not best forced into the Islamic/Christian category of “prophet,” because Sikhism possesses its own understanding of *Guru*. Nevertheless, Guru Nanak and the succeeding Gurus represent another powerful example of claimed knowledge of transcendent reality producing a continuing religious lineage. The Guru Granth Sahib eventually becomes the enduring Guru and contains not only Sikh Gurus but compositions associated with Hindu and Muslim holy figures. ([Wikisource][7])

9. **Later synthesizing prophetic movements.** Mani is particularly relevant to what we've been discussing. In the third century CE, he consciously presented his religion as completing earlier revelations and drew from Christianity, Iranian religion, and traditions encountered farther east. Manichaean tradition presented Mani as a recipient of revelation and a culminating messenger. ([Wikisource][8]) Much later, the Baháʼí tradition would explicitly construct a theology of successive divine messengers across different religions.

But there is a deeper distinction I think could become important to your project.

Human religious history may contain **several different kinds of claimed access to a transcendent realm**:

**Prophecy:** “Something beyond me spoke or communicated to me.”

**Vision:** “I saw something beyond ordinary perception.”

**Revelation:** “Knowledge was disclosed that I could not otherwise have known.”

**Illumination:** “I directly apprehended the nature of reality.”

**Mysticism:** “I experienced communion/unity/nearness with ultimate reality.”

**Divination:** “I obtained information from the divine realm through signs or procedures.”

**Shamanic mediation:** “I interacted with nonordinary beings or realms on behalf of others.”

Those phenomena occur in civilizations that sometimes had little or no contact with one another.

And **that** is potentially more interesting than simply cataloguing religions.

Suppose, for the sake of investigation, we temporarily adopt your hypothesis of a real **non-corporeal intelligence or transcendent intelligence** behind at least some religious experiences.

Then instead of asking:

> Which religion got God right?

we could ask:

> **Are there recurring characteristics across apparently independent human encounters with transcendent intelligence?**

That is potentially testable comparatively.

For example, we could take experiences attributed to:

**Abraham → Moses → Hebrew prophets → Zarathustra → Vedic rishis → Greek prophetic figures → Buddha → Jesus → Muhammad → Sikh Gurus → mystics and visionaries**

and remove the later institutional theology surrounding each one.

Then compare only the **earliest recoverable encounter reports and teachings**.

We could ask:

Who or what was encountered?

Was it described as personal or impersonal?

One or many?

Corporeal or non-corporeal?

Did it identify itself?

Did it communicate verbally?

Did it demand worship?

Did it demand ethical behavior?

Did it care about justice?

Was there judgment after death?

Did it claim to have created reality?

Did the experience occur spontaneously or through deliberate practice?

Did the recipient remain conscious?

Were there visions, voices, dreams, altered states, or ordinary waking encounters?

Did the messenger claim exclusive truth?

Did later followers substantially change the original teaching?

If completely independent traditions started producing a **common underlying pattern**, that would be much more interesting than discovering that later religions share doctrines because they borrowed them from one another.

And there is an intriguing Islamic connection to precisely this possibility. The Qur'an repeatedly asserts that divine messengers were sent to peoples beyond those named in the biblical genealogy—for example, that a messenger was sent to every community (Qur'an 16:36). Historically, that claim cannot establish that Zarathustra, the Vedic rishis, Buddha, or others actually were such messengers. But **within the theological framework you are exploring**, it provides a ready-made hypothesis:

**Abrahamic scripture itself may allow for genuine revelation occurring outside the conventionally recognized Abrahamic lineage.**

That could become a major principle of the religion we've been sketching: **the prophetic stream may not be one genealogical line at all. It may be multiple streams of human contact with the same ultimate reality, some preserved more accurately than others, converging and diverging throughout human history.**

## 1. First, “revelation” is several different phenomena

The evidence suggests at least five distinguishable encounter-types:

| Type                            | Basic claim                                                       | Examples                                 |
| ------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| **Messenger prophecy**          | “A divine being communicated something I must transmit.”          | Mari prophets, Hebrew prophets, Muhammad |
| **Prophetic dialogue**          | “I encountered/questioned the divine and received understanding.” | Moses traditions, Zoroaster              |
| **Inspired perception/hearing** | “I perceived/heard sacred truth.”                                 | Vedic ṛṣis                               |
| **Awakening/illumination**      | “I directly discovered the nature of reality.”                    | Buddha                                   |
| **Oracle/divination**           | “A deity or sacred process supplied information.”                 | Delphi, various ancient diviners         |

These shouldn't automatically be treated as equivalent.

At Old Babylonian Mari around the eighteenth century BCE, for example, roughly sixty-five surviving tablets attest men and women delivering revelations attributed to deities, often directed to the king. This is remarkably close structurally to biblical messenger-prophecy and predates the classical Hebrew prophets in surviving written evidence. ([JSTOR][1])

By contrast, the historical Buddha is represented primarily as someone who **discovers a path through awakening**, not someone commissioned by a creator God to deliver that God's speech. Early Buddhist teaching centers on suffering, its causes, liberation, the Middle Way, the Four Noble Truths and the Eightfold Path. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy][2])

That is a genuinely different epistemological claim.

# 3. There are genuine recurring patterns

Once we strip away later institutions, several themes recur with surprising frequency.

The most important are:

**A transcendent order exists.**

It may be called God, gods, *aša*, *ṛta*, Dharma, Tao, or something else—but humans repeatedly report that ordinary appearances are embedded within a greater order.

**Ordinary human perception is inadequate.**

Prophet, seer, sage and mystic each claim access to something most people normally fail to perceive.

**Human conduct matters.**

Truthfulness, justice, compassion, restraint, generosity, fidelity and avoidance of destructive appetites occur repeatedly.

**The human ego or ordinary desire is problematic.**

Hebrew prophecy condemns pride and exploitation.

Jesus repeatedly attacks status seeking, hypocrisy and attachment to wealth.

Islam emphasizes submission to God rather than self-will.

Buddhism analyzes craving as a fundamental cause of suffering.

Indian traditions develop extensive disciplines of detachment.

These aren't identical ideas, but the family resemblance is significant.

**Transformation is more important than mere intellectual assent.**

The prophet usually wants repentance.

The Buddha wants awakening.

The mystic wants purification.

The sage wants wisdom.

The religious goal isn't simply:

> believe proposition X.

It is:

> **become a different kind of human being.**

# 4. But the differences are just as important

This is where a simplistic “all religions say the same thing” model fails.

They plainly don't.

The Vedic texts contain many divine beings. ([OUP Academic][3])

Zoroaster's religious vision centers strongly on Ahura Mazda and moral cosmic opposition. ([Iranica Online][5])

Hebrew prophecy increasingly insists upon YHWH's sovereignty and righteousness.

Jesus operates inside Jewish monotheism while proclaiming the Kingdom of God; scholarly reconstruction consistently treats God's reign as central to his teaching. ([OUP Academic][6])

Muhammad's Qur'anic message insists on worship of God and rejection of false gods; notably, Qur'an 16:36 itself claims that a messenger was raised among **every community** with that basic message. ([Quranic Arabic Corpus][7])

The Buddha's original framework, on the other hand, doesn't require a creator God to explain liberation at all. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy][8])

Those differences cannot responsibly be erased.

# 5. We therefore need four competing hypotheses

This is where I think our inquiry becomes genuinely useful.

### Hypothesis A — One transcendent source

There is one ultimate intelligence/reality.

Different people encounter it through:

**language + culture + psychology + prior beliefs + historical circumstances**

so the received message becomes culturally filtered.

Something like:

**Reality → encounter → human consciousness → interpretation → oral transmission → text → institution.**

Under this hypothesis, disagreements arise increasingly farther down that chain.

This would fit what you have been suggesting.

### Hypothesis B — Multiple transcendent intelligences

Perhaps human beings have encountered **different non-corporeal intelligences**.

That would explain why some experiences describe dramatically different personalities and cosmologies.

Ancient religions themselves commonly entertained exactly this possibility.

Then the problem becomes distinguishing:

**ultimate God**

from

**lesser spiritual intelligences**

from

**human psychological phenomena.**

Interestingly, the Torah, Prophets, New Testament and Qur'an all already contain categories resembling this: God, angels/messengers, spirits, deceptive spirits, demons/jinn, etc.

### Hypothesis C — Human religious cognition

Religious experiences could originate primarily from characteristics of the human nervous system and culture.

Altered states, dreams, pattern recognition, social pressures, charismatic personalities and psychological phenomena could generate perceived divine encounters.

This hypothesis has to remain available if we're conducting the investigation seriously.

### Hypothesis D — Mixed model

This may actually be the most methodologically useful starting position.

Some experiences might be:

**genuine transcendence**

while others are:

**psychological experiences**

and others:

**fraud**

and others:

**later legendary development**

and still others:

**mixtures of all of these.**

Nothing requires one explanation to account for every religious claim ever made.

> **Judge claims individually rather than validating or rejecting entire religions wholesale.**

That could become enormously important for the religion you're contemplating.

> “Is Christianity true?”

Instead:

> Did Jesus most likely teach this particular proposition?

Then:

> Is that proposition credible?

Likewise we wouldn't ask:

> “Is Islam true?”

Same with Moses, Isaiah, Zarathustra, Buddha, Nanak, the Vedic seers, etc.

That means a tradition might preserve enormous amounts of genuine insight while simultaneously containing later misunderstanding.

# 7. Source proximity becomes crucial

This dramatically changes how we weigh evidence.

For **Jesus**, our evidence consists primarily of early Christian writings rather than writings authored by Jesus. The New Testament documents themselves are theological works, which complicates reconstruction, although historians can still identify major features of Jesus' career and message. ([Unvis][9])

For **Muhammad**, the situation is different. The Qur'an itself belongs very near the founding period and increasingly early manuscript evidence supports an early Qur'anic text, whereas extensive narrative biographies and hadith collections were written substantially later. Current scholarship therefore distinguishes strongly between the Qur'an as an early source and later literary traditions about Muhammad. ([Cambridge University Press][10])

For **Zoroaster**, the Gathas are unusually valuable because scholars often associate them closely with Zarathustra himself, notwithstanding substantial unresolved questions. ([Iranica Online][11])

For **Buddha**, the preserved collections are later than his lifetime, so historical reconstruction again requires extracting common early strata.

Thus we should assign every teaching a **provenance score**, not merely attach the founder's name to it.

Yes. In fact, once we widen the category beyond “prophets,” there appears to be a **global history of humans claiming contact with realities or intelligences beyond ordinary sensory experience**.

What does **not** exist is one universally accepted scholarly narrative called *The Global History of Human Contact with Transcendent Reality*. The evidence is normally divided among archaeology, anthropology, history of religions, ancient history, psychology, textual criticism, and ethnography. But those pieces can be assembled into such a history.

The crucial methodological distinction would be:

> **We can establish historically that humans claimed these encounters. Historical evidence alone cannot establish that the beings or realities encountered objectively existed.**

That distinction would allow us to investigate the phenomenon seriously without deciding beforehand either “all of this was supernatural” or “all of this was hallucination.”

### The history seems to begin before writing

The oldest part is necessarily uncertain.

Upper Paleolithic caves, unusual burials, ritual objects, therianthropic human-animal images, deliberately difficult-to-access sacred spaces, and other archaeological evidence suggest that people tens of thousands of years ago possessed elaborate symbolic worlds. Some archaeologists have proposed shamanic or altered-consciousness interpretations of Paleolithic cave art, although these interpretations remain contested; we cannot interview the artists or recover their theology. ([Smithsonian Magazine][1])

So our first chronological category would have to be:

**c. 50,000–10,000 BCE — probable ritual/transcendent consciousness, but essentially no recoverable propositions.**

We can reasonably infer things such as ritual, symbolic thought, treatment of death and perhaps concepts of unseen agency. We generally cannot say:

> “This Paleolithic person believed God told him X.”

There simply isn't enough evidence.

### Then writing suddenly lets us hear what humans thought the unseen world was saying

By the Bronze Age, things change dramatically.

Ancient Mesopotamia left enormous amounts of evidence for attempts to communicate with, or discern the intentions of, gods. Dreams, omens, celestial phenomena, animal entrails and direct prophetic messages could all function as channels of divine information.

Clay liver models such as examples excavated at Mari were used to teach or record **hepatoscopy**—reading sacrificial livers for signs. Mesopotamian scholars compiled huge omen series based on terrestrial and celestial phenomena.

Even more interesting are the Mari prophetic texts from the early second millennium BCE. They describe real individuals—men and women—who claimed that particular deities had communicated messages intended for the king.

That puts us unmistakably in:

> **“A nonhuman intelligence communicated something to me that I am supposed to tell another human.”**

Structurally, that is remarkably close to later prophecy.

### Egypt develops another enormous encounter tradition

Egyptian religion gives us gods communicating through dreams, temple oracles, ritual, divine images and eventually highly developed oracular systems.

The pharaoh himself existed partly as an intermediary between human society and divine order.

Again, though, this differs from Hebrew prophecy. The fundamental structure is usually **maintenance of cosmic order and relationship with numerous divine beings**, rather than one transcendent God repeatedly commissioning moral reformers.

### Shang China gives us extraordinary physical evidence

By about **1200 BCE**, Chinese royal diviners were recording questions on turtle plastrons and animal bones.

These are the famous **oracle bones**, and they preserve some of the earliest securely recognizable Chinese writing. The inscriptions record royal divination in sophisticated detail. ([British Museum][2])

Questions concerned things like:

rain,

harvest,

war,

illness,

childbirth,

hunting,

ancestral intentions,

and royal affairs.

So ancient China gives us another independent-looking system in which humans believed that information could pass between the ordinary human world and an invisible ancestral/divine realm.

This is very important to our investigation because Shang China is geographically and culturally far removed from Israelite prophecy.

### Indigenous traditions make the phenomenon genuinely global

And once ethnographic evidence becomes available, the geographic distribution becomes enormous.

Across numerous societies in Africa, ritual specialists are understood to communicate with spirits, ancestors or supernatural powers through possession, dreams and divination.

For example, the Metropolitan Museum describes traditional African diviners as trained mediators with spiritual and ancestral realms and documents systems from dozens of African cultures. ([The Metropolitan Museum of Art][3])

Among Luba diviners, possession can function as a means through which spirits answer inquiries. ([Metropolitan Museum of Art Resources][4])

Among Baule traditions, diviners may enter trance and are understood to communicate with nature spirits. ([The Metropolitan Museum of Art][5])

Among Senufo communities, divination explicitly concerns communication between humans and nature spirits. ([The Metropolitan Museum of Art][6])

Yoruba **Ifá** constitutes yet another highly developed system, with trained specialists using an extensive symbolic corpus to seek information concerning destiny. ([The Metropolitan Museum of Art][7])

These traditions aren't interchangeable, but the general phenomenon—**specialists mediating between visible and invisible realms**—recurs.

### The Americas independently show the same broad phenomenon

Pre-Columbian Mesoamerican evidence likewise includes priests, diviners, ritual specialists, deity impersonation, ancestor contact, visionary experiences and attempts to communicate with supernatural powers.

The Met describes ritual specialists in western Mesoamerica conducting initiatory rites that facilitated contact with supernatural beings and ancestors. ([The Metropolitan Museum of Art][8])

Among the Maya, ceremonial music, prayer, food, drink and ritual objects functioned as channels for communication with divine powers; other evidence explicitly describes worshippers calling upon divine beings for supernatural intervention. ([The Metropolitan Museum of Art][9])

Native North American traditions contain still other forms of visionary contact. Different nations have very different religious systems, so “Native American religion” cannot responsibly be treated as one religion. But traditions involving visions, dreams, spirits, healing specialists and journeys into supernatural realms occur across many societies. The Met's comparative material describes shamans entering trance and understanding themselves to communicate directly with supernatural beings. ([Metropolitan Museum of Art Resources][10])

That geographic independence matters.

### Australia may preserve one of humanity's longest continuous sacred traditions

Australian Aboriginal traditions involve what English speakers commonly call **the Dreaming**, although Indigenous languages contain many more precise concepts.

These traditions connect landscape, ancestral beings, law, ritual, song, identity and sacred history. Some oral traditions appear capable of preserving memories of geological events across astonishing spans of time, though individual claims require careful evaluation. ([Smithsonian Magazine][11])

So here again we encounter a worldview in which ordinary physical reality and ancestral/sacred reality aren't separated in the modern Western fashion.

## Then something interesting happens during the late Bronze and Iron Ages

Several traditions begin producing **highly articulated accounts of transcendent truth** rather than simply divination.

This is where our earlier “prophetic/revelatory streams” become particularly useful.

In India, the **Vedic ṛṣis** understand themselves as perceivers or hearers of sacred truth.

In ancient Iran, **Zarathustra** addresses Ahura Mazda and portrays himself as receiving profound religious insight concerning truth, falsehood, morality and humanity's relationship to the divine. The Gathas repeatedly address Ahura Mazda; Zoroastrian tradition came to understand Zarathustra as a conduit of revelation. ([Iranica Online][12])

In Israel, the prophetic tradition develops the characteristic:

> “Thus says YHWH…”

model.

And the focus increasingly becomes not:

> “Will we win tomorrow's battle?”

but:

> **“You are behaving unjustly; repent.”**

That is an important transition.

Transcendent communication becomes increasingly **ethical**.

---

## Greece produces another form

Ancient Greek religion develops institutionalized oracles, most famously the **Pythia at Delphi**, who was understood to deliver Apollo's responses.

So Greece again contains the basic structure:

**human question → altered/ritual mediation → purported divine communication → human interpretation.**

But Greek oracular religion generally does not develop the same linear sequence of moral-revelatory prophets characteristic of Israel.

---

## Then the phenomenon changes again

Beginning roughly in the first millennium BCE, we encounter figures whose central claim isn't necessarily:

> “A god spoke to me.”

but:

> **“I have perceived the nature of reality.”**

The Buddha is the clearest example.

His authority rests fundamentally upon **awakening** rather than commission by a creator deity.

That gives us an entirely different category of purported transcendent contact:

**revelation from an intelligence**

versus

**direct apprehension of ultimate reality**.

Those could conceivably be completely different phenomena—or different interpretations of related altered states.

That is one of the questions our investigation should leave open.

# Jesus presents an unusual hybrid

Jesus belongs unmistakably within the Jewish prophetic world but doesn't fit neatly into the ordinary prophetic category.

In the earliest traditions he:

prays to God,

speaks about God,

announces God's kingdom,

claims unusual authority,

has visionary experiences,

and is represented as possessing an exceptionally intimate relationship with the one he calls **Father**.

Later Christianity makes considerably larger metaphysical claims about him.

For our study, we would initially separate:

**the historically recoverable religious experience and teachings of Jesus**

from

**subsequent Christian explanations of who Jesus metaphysically was.**

That distinction is precisely the one you raised earlier.

---

# Muhammad returns explicitly to revelation

Muhammad represents one of history's clearest claims of sustained revelatory communication.

The Qur'an repeatedly represents its contents as communication originating with God rather than Muhammad.

And particularly relevant to our investigation, the Qur'an itself proposes something resembling a **global history of revelation**.

It claims that messengers were sent among peoples beyond the biblical lineage, including the sweeping statement of Qur'an 16:36 that a messenger was raised among every community.

That doesn't historically prove such a global revelation occurred.

But it is striking that **Islam's own theology explicitly leaves room for unnamed prophets throughout human history**.

Under that model, figures outside the biblical genealogy could potentially represent authentic encounters without being identified by the Qur'an.

---

# Sikhism provides another fascinating later case

Guru Nanak appears in a religious environment where Hindu and Islamic traditions already coexist.

His teaching emphasizes a single transcendent divine reality, devotion, remembrance, ethical life and the inadequacy of purely external religious distinctions.

It therefore gives us an excellent later case for examining whether religious experience can transcend inherited religious categories without simply becoming an indiscriminate mixture.

---

# Mysticism creates yet another global layer

Then there is an enormous class of experiences that doesn't primarily involve messages.

Christian mystics,

Jewish Kabbalists,

Muslim Sufis,

Hindu contemplatives,

Buddhist meditators,

Daoist practitioners,

Sikh mystics,

Indigenous ritual specialists,

and others report experiences described in terms such as:

**presence,**

**unity,**

**light,**

**emptiness,**

**love,**

**loss of ego,**

**timelessness,**

**knowledge beyond language,**

or **direct encounter with ultimate reality**.

What makes this category particularly interesting is that the reported phenomenology sometimes resembles itself across traditions even when the **interpretation** differs radically.

A Christian might say:

> “I experienced God.”

A Hindu might say:

> “I apprehended Brahman.”

A Buddhist might resist interpreting the experience as encountering a being at all.

That distinction between **experience** and **interpretation of experience** may turn out to be one of the most important concepts in our project.

Once we put everything on the table, humans seem repeatedly to report about **five basic kinds of contact**:

| Encounter                           | Claim                                          |

And those categories appear in **widely separated cultures**.

That fact itself is real.

What explains it is another matter.

## There are several serious explanations

We shouldn't prematurely choose between them.

**Common neurology** could generate similar experiences because all humans possess similar brains.

**Cultural transmission** explains some similarities because civilizations have exchanged religious ideas for thousands of years.

**Convergent psychology** could cause unrelated societies to produce similar religious structures.

**Social function** could explain specialists such as shamans, diviners and prophets.

**Altered states of consciousness** produced through fasting, prayer, meditation, sleep deprivation, chanting, psychoactive substances, illness or trauma could produce recurring phenomenology.

**Actual non-corporeal intelligences** could produce at least some experiences.

**An ultimate transcendent reality** could be encountered differently through different human conceptual systems.

Or several of those explanations could be true simultaneously.

That last possibility deserves particular attention.

There is no reason, logically, that **every claimed supernatural encounter must have the same cause**.

Someone could fabricate a revelation.

Another person could hallucinate.

Another could misinterpret a dream.

Another could undergo an unusual but natural neurological experience.

And another—if transcendent reality actually exists—could genuinely encounter it.

A sufficiently rigorous religion would need to allow all five possibilities.

Agreed. That gives the project a much firmer foundation because it does **not begin by deciding which religion is correct**. It begins with the human record of claimed contact with transcendent reality and lets the comparative evidence shape whatever comes afterward.

I’d structure it in four major phases.

1. **Build the encounter corpus.** Collect the earliest and strongest evidence for prophecy, revelation, visions, mystical experience, divination, possession, illumination, sacred dreams, and related phenomena across cultures and periods.

2. **Separate encounter from interpretation.** For every case, distinguish:

   * what the person reportedly experienced,
   * what they thought it meant,
   * what immediate followers said,
   * what later institutions eventually taught.

3. **Compare the phenomena systematically.** Use the same questions for every stream: What was encountered? Personal or impersonal? One or many? Did it communicate? Was there moral content? Did it demand worship? Was the experience spontaneous or induced? Did later teachings amplify or alter it?

4. **Only then construct theology.** After the comparative work, ask whether the evidence best supports one transcendent source, multiple intelligences, recurrent human cognitive phenomena, some combination of these, or something else entirely.

The most important methodological principle should probably be:

> **No tradition receives automatic authority because of age, popularity, institutional continuity, or its own claims. Every claim is evaluated according to provenance, source proximity, historical context, internal coherence, independent attestation, and comparative evidence.**

And we should keep another distinction permanently visible:

**event → report → interpretation → transmission → scripture → commentary → institution → doctrine**

Those are not the same thing.

For the actual research order, I would start with the **best-attested ancient cases**, because they give us the strongest baseline for comparison: Mesopotamian prophecy and divination, Egyptian divine communication, Shang oracle traditions, Vedic seers, Zoroaster, early Israelite prophecy, Greek oracles, early Buddhist awakening claims, Jesus, Muhammad, and then major indigenous and mystical traditions where the evidence allows responsible reconstruction.

The end product could eventually have three layers:

**Historical corpus** — what humans claimed happened.

**Comparative analysis** — recurring patterns, contradictions, possible dependencies, and alternative explanations.

**Constructive theology** — what conclusions, if any, are justified after the evidence is weighed.

That keeps the eventual religion, philosophy, or spiritual framework from being something we simply invent first and defend afterward.

The next move should be to create the **master research framework and candidate corpus**: probably 25–40 traditions or figures, each evaluated with the exact same evidentiary template.
