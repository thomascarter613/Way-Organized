Yes. The strongest way to protect the project is not to argue that it is “not a cult,” but to **design it so that the mechanisms normally associated with high-control religious groups are structurally difficult or impossible to develop**.

That distinction matters because “cult” does not have a settled scholarly definition and is often used polemically. Contemporary scholarship is much more useful when it examines observable things such as coercive control, isolation, charismatic authority, exclusivism, concealment, and abuse. ([Frontiers][1])

For this project, I would establish an explicit **Anti-Coercion and Institutional Integrity Architecture** from the beginning.

### The foundational principle

I would put something very close to this into the constitution:

> **No human being, office, council, text, claimed revelation, or institution possesses authority to compel another person's conscience.**

That should become constitutional rather than merely aspirational.

For our particular project, there is an additional principle that fits extremely well with the direction we have already taken: **encounter, interpretation, theology, and institutional authority must remain distinguishable**. A claimed encounter with transcendent reality is evidence to be investigated; it is not automatically a command everyone else must obey.

That means even if someone someday reports an extraordinary revelation, the institutional response should be:

**“That is a claim to be examined.”**

Never:

**“God has spoken through this person; therefore everyone must obey.”**

That single distinction could become one of the most important safeguards in the religion.

### I would make these constitutional non-negotiables

* **No infallible living leader.** No founder, prophet, president, imam, priest, teacher, elder, council, or successor is beyond criticism, removal, correction, or investigation.

* **No founder supremacy.** The founder should not be able to appoint/remove the governing board unilaterally, determine his own compensation, adjudicate complaints against himself, control organizational assets personally, or designate an unquestionable successor.

* **Independent governance.** I would use at least 7 directors, with at least two-thirds independent of staff, family relationships, major financial relationships, and one another. ECFA's existing benchmark, although designed for evangelical organizations rather than our project, requires at least five directors and a majority-independent board; its rationale is specifically preventing undue influence and conflicts of interest. ([ECFA][2])

* **A Member Bill of Rights.** Every person should explicitly retain the right to leave; disagree; criticize leadership; read opposing literature; associate with nonmembers; maintain family relationships; obtain outside legal, medical, psychological, financial, and religious advice; refuse donations; decline activities; retain personal property and income; and report misconduct without retaliation.

* **No shunning requirement.** Leaving the religion, changing one's theology, or criticizing leadership should never require family or friends to sever ordinary human relationships with that person. Organizational positions can have legitimate membership qualifications; personal relationships should not become instruments of enforcement.

* **No coercive revelation.** A leader may never use “God told me,” “the Spirit revealed,” “the council discerned,” or equivalent language to override someone's consent regarding marriage, sex, medical treatment, finances, employment, residence, education, voting, family relationships, or other personal decisions.

* **Radical financial transparency.** Publish an annual budget, actual expenditures, leadership compensation, related-party transactions, major asset holdings, and independently prepared financial statements. Churches generally are not required to file annual Form 990 returns with the IRS, which makes voluntary disclosure especially meaningful. ([IRS][3]) Leadership compensation should be approved by disinterested decision-makers using external comparability information; this also corresponds with IRS guidance concerning reasonable compensation and excess-benefit transactions. ([IRS][4])

* **No purchase of spiritual standing.** Donations, tithes, property transfers, volunteer labor, or bequests must never purchase authority, salvation, revelation, initiation, preferred spiritual status, or access to leadership.

* **Independent safeguarding and complaints.** Complaints concerning senior leadership should bypass senior leadership entirely. Abuse allegations should be capable of referral to genuinely independent investigators, and criminal allegations must go to appropriate civil authorities rather than being treated merely as internal religious matters. GRACE, for example, emphasizes that independent organizational investigations do not substitute for criminal investigations. ([GRACE][5])

* **Strong child and vulnerable-person protections.** Background checks where appropriate, two-adult practices, reporting procedures, restrictions on private adult/minor contact, safeguarding training, written boundaries for counseling, and mandatory compliance with applicable reporting laws should exist before children's programs begin.

* **No control monopoly.** Ideally the same religious authority should not simultaneously control someone's theology, housing, job, income, schooling, healthcare, marriage, and social network. If communal living ever becomes part of the movement, housing and employment arrangements should have ordinary written contracts, exit procedures, property rights, and independent dispute resolution rather than becoming mechanisms for enforcing religious obedience.

* **No secret inner doctrine that contradicts the public doctrine.** There can certainly be advanced education, initiatory ritual, private pastoral conversations, and confidential matters. But outsiders and new members should not be deliberately shown a benign version of the religion while insiders are gradually subjected to materially different obligations.

### The founder question is especially important

New movements frequently begin with one unusually motivated person. That is not inherently problematic. The structural danger appears when **founder → visionary → interpreter → executive → treasurer → disciplinarian → final theological authority** all become the same office.

We should deliberately separate them.

You could remain the founder and intellectual architect without becoming an unquestionable religious authority. In fact, I think the project's intellectual premise gives us an unusually good opportunity to establish this very early.

Instead of:

**Founder → revelation → doctrine → followers**

we can build:

**Evidence → investigation → interpretation → deliberation → provisional conclusions → continuing inquiry**

The founder participates in that system but is not the epistemic endpoint.

This is particularly appropriate for our historical-phenomenological approach to reported encounters with transcendent reality. Our starting proposition is essentially that humanity has produced a vast body of claims deserving systematic examination—not that Thomas Carter has received the final explanation for them.

### We should also institutionalize theological humility

I'd actually make this part of the religion's theology.

For example:

**Reality is greater than our description of reality.**

Therefore:

**Experience can be genuine while interpretation can be mistaken.**

Therefore:

**No claimed revelation becomes universally binding merely because someone reports it.**

Therefore:

**Doctrines can possess different confidence levels.**

We could formally classify teachings something like:

**observed evidence → historical inference → philosophical interpretation → theological hypothesis → communal tradition → personal belief.**

That prevents the organization from quietly converting speculation into absolute revelation.

And we should explicitly allow:

**“We don't know.”**

A religion that institutionalizes uncertainty where evidence does not justify certainty creates an important barrier against authoritarian theology.

### Transparency should go far beyond finances

I would eventually publish on the organization's website its constitution and bylaws, governing-board membership, leadership-selection process, compensation-setting procedure, conflicts-of-interest policy, annual financial report, Member Bill of Rights, safeguarding policy, complaint procedure, discipline and appeals procedure, doctrinal-development procedure, historical research methodology, substantial doctrinal revisions, and leadership-removal process.

Visitors should be able to discover **how the organization could remove its own founder**.

That would be extraordinarily powerful.

Likewise, they should be able to discover exactly how to file a complaint against the most powerful person in the organization without that person learning the complainant's identity prematurely or controlling the investigation.

Transparency and independent governance are recognized accountability principles in established religious nonprofits; ECFA specifically treats independent board governance, financial oversight, transparency, compensation integrity, and conflict-of-interest controls as core stewardship standards. ([ECFA][6])

### There are some practices I would prohibit outright

No forced or manipulated confessions.

No sexual access to members as a privilege of leadership.

No leader/member sexual relationship where there is pastoral or supervisory power without removing that power relationship first.

No sleep deprivation, food deprivation, prolonged ritual, psychedelic use, social pressure, or emotional exhaustion as a method of obtaining commitment.

No discouraging medical treatment.

No confiscation of passports, identification, phones, money, or correspondence.

No requirement to obtain permission to leave organizational property.

No threats of supernatural punishment targeted at people for questioning leadership.

No teaching that critics are necessarily evil, possessed, apostate, enemies, spiritually contaminated, or agents of some conspiracy merely because they criticize the organization.

No requirement that members cut off outsiders.

No surveillance of members' private lives except ordinary safeguarding or organizational requirements.

No disciplinary proceedings where the accuser, investigator, prosecutor, and adjudicator are effectively the same person.

No irrevocable vows of financial dependence.

And no doctrine that makes the continued existence or authority of the institution necessary for God/transcendent reality to remain accessible to humanity.

That last one is important.

The organization should be able to say:

> **If this institution disappeared tomorrow, truth would remain truth.**

That is almost the opposite of institutional narcissism.

### I would go one step further: invite adversarial examination

Once established, periodically invite an outside nonprofit attorney, accountant, safeguarding professional, scholar of religion, and perhaps someone with expertise in coercive-control/high-control organizations to inspect the institution.

Tell them:

**“Try to find how this organization could become abusive.”**

Then publish their findings and the response.

You could even require an **Institutional Integrity Review every three years**.

That changes accountability from:

> “Trust us.”

to:

> “Here are the mechanisms through which you can verify us.”

### And there is a cultural test that may be even more important than the documents

Imagine a member stands up during a meeting and says:

> “Thomas is wrong about this.”

What happens?

If the institutional instinct is curiosity—“Tell us why”—the organization is probably functioning properly.

If the instinct becomes “How dare you question the founder?”, something has gone badly wrong regardless of what the bylaws say.

Likewise, imagine someone says:

> “I no longer believe this religion is true, and I'm leaving.”

The ideal response should be something like:

> “You are free to go. You remain our friend. Your dignity, relationships, property, reputation, and privacy remain yours.”

That principle should survive even painful departures.

That would do considerably more than help the religion *look* unlike a high-control group. It would deliberately engineer the institution so that becoming one would require dismantling publicly visible constitutional safeguards first—which members, directors, outsiders, and future generations could recognize and resist.
