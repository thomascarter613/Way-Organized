For a new church, I would distinguish the **legal minimum the IRS publishes** from the **operating standard I would build before asking the IRS for a determination letter**. There is no published IRS rule saying “you need 20 people,” “you must meet every Sunday,” or “you must operate for one year.” The current Form 1023 instructions instead use a facts-and-circumstances analysis and focus heavily on whether an **established congregation actually exists and regularly participates in religious services at an established location**. ([IRS][1])

### A defensible minimum viable congregation

I would build toward something approximately like this:

| Element              | IRS minimum stated?                              | Practical target I would use                                     |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Congregants          | No numerical minimum                             | **10–20 unrelated adults**, plus children/families if applicable |
| Families represented | No numerical minimum                             | **At least 5–10 households**                                     |
| Service frequency    | Must be “regular”                                | **Weekly**                                                       |
| Established location | Yes, strongly relevant                           | Same location or a clearly recurring reserved location           |
| Operating history    | No stated minimum                                | **6 months minimum; 9–12 months preferable**                     |
| Membership           | No stated minimum                                | Formal roster with a real admission process                      |
| Attendance records   | IRS asks for average attendance                  | Keep a record for every gathering                                |
| Minister             | Ordained ministers are one church characteristic | At least one genuinely trained/credentialed religious leader     |
| Worship form         | IRS expressly asks about it                      | Adopt and consistently use an identifiable order of worship      |
| Rites                | Not every church must have identical rites       | Establish rites appropriate to the faith                         |
| Religious teaching   | Important church characteristic                  | Adult teaching plus youth instruction when children participate  |
| Financial operations | §501(c)(3) requirement                           | Church bank account, accounting, contributions, receipts, budget |
| Governance           | Church characteristic                            | Functioning board/council/elders rather than paper positions     |

The numbers in the right column are **my risk-reduction recommendations, not IRS thresholds**. The IRS does not publish a minimum congregation size. What it does say is particularly revealing: an established congregation “generally doesn't include members of only one family.” ([IRS][1])

So I would not try to establish church status with, for example, just you, your spouse and your child. Even if the religious activity were sincere, that fact pattern runs directly into something Schedule A tells the examining agent to consider.

## What I would actually establish

Imagine that we formed the corporation and then began services.

Every Saturday, Sunday, or whatever day the religion designates, the organization holds a service at:

> Community Room A
> Every Sunday
> 10:00 a.m.–11:30 a.m.

The facility could be rented for a few hours each week. It could also be provided free. The IRS explicitly says an established place of worship may be **owned, rented, or provided free for the organization's use**. ([IRS][1])

There is therefore **no need to buy a church building**.

The organization livestreams the same service. Someone hundreds of miles away can participate online. But the physical gathering gives us an unmistakable established congregation while the online ministry allows the community to grow without geographical limitation.

Over time, those remote members could establish additional local congregations.

### The service should be recognizably religious worship

The IRS isn't entitled to dictate the theology or require Christianity-style worship. But Schedule A specifically asks you to provide the **regular days and times of services**, describe the **order of events during the service**, and explain how those activities further the organization's religious purposes. ([IRS][1])

So we should actually have an adopted liturgy/order of service. For example:

1. Gathering/opening invocation
2. Reading from sacred/religious texts
3. Prayer, meditation or contemplative practice
4. Hymn, chant or other devotional practice
5. Religious teaching/homily
6. Congregational discussion or response
7. Religious rite where appropriate
8. Community prayers/intercessions
9. Offering
10. Benediction/dismissal
11. Fellowship/community time

That is only an example. Our eventual religious framework should determine the actual content rather than constructing theology around an IRS checklist.

What matters evidentially is that **the written worship order and what people really do agree with one another**.

## Membership should be real

This is another area where I would be formal from the beginning.

The IRS Schedule A asks:

* total number of members;
* whether there is a prescribed way to become a member;
* what actions are required to become one;
* members' rights and benefits;
* whether there are different membership levels;
* whether members may simultaneously belong to another church or denomination. ([IRS][2])

So I would create an actual membership system.

Something such as:

**Participant → Catechumen/Seeker → Member → Minister/Officer**

Not necessarily those names, but those conceptual states.

Membership might require:

> participation in several services → orientation/instruction → affirmation of the community's foundational covenant → voluntary request for membership → acceptance according to the governance rules → entry into the membership register.

The member register then records:

* member number;
* name;
* date joined;
* membership status;
* membership category;
* date departed, if applicable.

We shouldn't create meaningless complexity just to impress the IRS. But there should be an objective way of answering, **“Who actually belongs to this religious community?”**

## Attendance records become unusually useful evidence

For every worship service, I would preserve:

**Service Record — SR-YYYY-MM-DD**

containing:

* date;
* beginning/end time;
* location;
* presiding minister;
* other participating clergy/leaders;
* members physically present;
* nonmembers physically present;
* online participants separately counted;
* readings;
* sermon/teaching title;
* rites performed;
* offering received;
* notable events.

The IRS expressly asks for the **average number of members and nonmembers attending regularly scheduled services**. ([IRS][1])

After six months we therefore wouldn't be guessing:

> “About 15 people attend.”

We could state something like:

> During the 26 regularly scheduled weekly services conducted from March 1 through August 23, average physical attendance was 17.8 persons, consisting of an average of 13.2 members and 4.6 nonmembers.

And we'd possess 26 service records supporting it.

That is vastly better evidence.

## I would distinguish physical and online attendance

This is important.

Don't combine:

**11 people physically present + 93 YouTube viewers = attendance of 104.**

Instead preserve separate categories:

**Physical attendance:** 11
**Synchronous online participants:** 14
**Recorded-stream views:** 79

That makes the record transparent and avoids creating an appearance that we're trying to turn a media audience into a congregation.

The physical 11 are the clearest evidence of an established congregational gathering.

The 14 live remote participants can still be part of the broader religious community.

The 79 later views demonstrate religious outreach but shouldn't be represented as congregational attendance.

## There is an especially interesting opportunity here

We don't actually need to create a traditional American Protestant model unless that is what the religion ultimately becomes.

The IRS's term **“church” is generic**. Its own guidance expressly recognizes mosques, temples and synagogues and says other forms of religious organizations may also qualify. ([IRS][2])

So the institution we've been discussing could have:

**a global religious body**
↓
**local congregations/assemblies**
↓
**household or neighborhood groups**
↓
**individual members**

For example:

**The Communion / Order / Assembly**
→ East Tennessee Congregation
→ Knoxville Congregation
→ Nashville Congregation
→ Online Community

Those aren't proposed names—just architecture.

That organizational model actually fits very naturally with what we've been developing: a religious tradition emerging from systematic study of humanity's claimed encounters with transcendent reality rather than simply forming another conventional denomination.

## Ministers need considerably more thought

One of the fourteen traditional characteristics is an organization of ordained ministers, and another is ministers selected after completing a prescribed course of study. The current instructions specifically clarify that the required study may be **formal or informal**, but it does not include simply self-ordaining or paying for an ordination certificate without completing substantive training. ([IRS][2])

That is extremely useful for us.

We can build our **own ministerial formation program**.

For example:

**Ministerial Formation I — Foundations**

* history of religion;
* phenomenology of religious experience;
* comparative scripture;
* theology/philosophy of religion;
* ethics.

**Formation II — Tradition**

* doctrine;
* sacred literature;
* ritual;
* liturgy;
* community rule;
* spiritual disciplines.

**Formation III — Ministry**

* pastoral care;
* teaching;
* conducting worship;
* weddings/funerals/initiation rites;
* confidentiality;
* safeguarding;
* nonprofit/church administration.

**Formation IV — Practicum**

* supervised worship leadership;
* teaching;
* pastoral service;
* written examination;
* oral examination.

Then:

**candidate → licensed minister → ordained minister**

with genuine records of completion.

That doesn't merely satisfy an IRS characteristic. It gives the religious tradition institutional continuity beyond its founder.

## Rites matter too

Schedule A explicitly asks whether the organization conducts:

**baptisms, weddings, funerals, or other religious rites.** ([IRS][2])

“Other religious rites” is important. We are not required to imitate Christian sacraments.

Our eventual system might develop rites for:

* initiation;
* admission to the community;
* coming of age;
* marriage;
* birth/naming;
* death/funeral;
* ordination;
* reconciliation;
* annual observances;
* pilgrimage;
* communal meals;
* contemplative initiation.

Again, these should arise from actual theology and religious practice—not be manufactured merely to obtain tax status.

## Children and ministerial education are secondary, not fatal

Two of the traditional fourteen characteristics are:

**religious instruction for the young**, and
**schools for preparing ministers.**

But the IRS specifically says an organization does **not have to possess every characteristic** to be classified as a church. ([IRS][3])

And “Sunday school” doesn't literally require Sunday school. The Form 1023 instructions describe it as **regularly scheduled religious educational activities for youth**. ([IRS][2])

So eventually we could have something much more appropriate to the tradition.

## How long before filing?

There is **no IRS rule requiring six months or a year**.

Indeed, Form 1023 accommodates newly organized entities and asks for projected financial information where operating history doesn't yet exist.

But I would separate:

**“When may we legally file?”**

from

**“When will we have a compelling evidentiary record?”**

I would probably target:

**Month 0 — corporation established**
**Month 1 — organizational documents adopted**
**Month 1 — membership opened**
**Month 1 — regular worship begins**
**Months 1–6 — congregation operates**
**Month 3 — first quarterly congregational report**
**Month 6 — second quarterly report and evidence audit**
**Months 6–9 — Form 1023 preparation/submission**

If circumstances justified it, we could file sooner. But six months gives us roughly **26 weekly services** rather than merely telling the IRS what we intend to do.

A full year would give approximately **52 services**, annual religious observances, financial history, membership changes, ministry records and possibly rites such as marriages, funerals or initiations.

That is substantially more persuasive.

### What I would want before pressing “Submit”

I would want to be able to say, truthfully:

> We are a legally constituted religious body with an adopted creed, doctrine, discipline and ecclesiastical government. We have an established congregation composed of multiple unrelated households. The congregation meets every week at an established place of worship. We maintain a defined membership process and membership register. Ordained ministers conduct the services. Our ministers complete a prescribed program of religious formation. We conduct regular worship according to an established form, perform religious rites, provide religious education, maintain our own religious literature, and have documented these activities continuously since [date].

Then behind each sentence would be an exhibit proving it.

That is the real objective.

And there is a useful fallback built directly into IRS guidance: if an organization is genuinely religious but **doesn't yet have an established congregation**, the IRS says it may instead be a religious organization that does not presently qualify as a church and may **request church classification later after establishing the congregation**, including through Form 8940. ([IRS][1])

So we don't have to fake maturity at formation.

The next step I'd take is to turn this into a **12-month Church Establishment Program**: week-by-week from incorporation → governance → doctrine → first members → first worship service → ministerial formation → rites → recordkeeping → quarterly evidence audits → completed Form 1023/Schedule A package. That would connect directly to the **54-deliverable church qualification binder** we outlined earlier.
