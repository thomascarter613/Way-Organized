Based on what you’ve provided, I think we can now resolve most of the previously open questions. I would treat the following as our **provisional founding decisions** unless you object.

1. **Corporate form: Tennessee public-benefit religious nonprofit corporation.** Tennessee requires the charter to identify the corporation as either public-benefit or mutual-benefit and, if applicable, as religious. Because the organization is intended for religious, educational, charitable, research, and other public purposes and will seek §501(c)(3) status, **public-benefit religious corporation** is the natural fit. ([Justia Law][1])

2. **Statutory members: no statutory members at formation.** This is my recommendation. “No statutory members” does **not** mean the religion cannot have members, adherents, congregants, participants, local communities, etc. It means those people do not automatically become corporate-law voters with the powers Tennessee gives statutory members. The board can then be selected under the charter/bylaws rather than creating a legal electorate before we even know what the community looks like. Tennessee expressly allows a nonmember corporation to specify how directors are selected. ([Justia Law][2])

   This is particularly appropriate here because we can give participants extraordinarily strong constitutional rights—the Bill of Rights, complaint rights, transparency rights, anti-shunning protections, etc.—without turning every religious membership question into a corporate voting question. After perhaps **2–3 years of actual community operation**, we can deliberately reconsider whether some form of statutory membership would improve the polity.

3. **Board: you remain Founder and one initial director, but we now need other humans.** Tennessee requires **at least three natural-person directors**, so we need at least two additional people before the corporation has a legally compliant board. ([Justia Law][3]) My governance recommendation remains stronger: **target five formation directors, then seven within roughly 180 days**, with at least two-thirds independent.

   I would therefore recruit **four additional people if possible**, but we can proceed legally once we have two suitable additional directors.

   An independent director, for our purposes, should not be your spouse or close relative, your employee, financially dependent on you, a major business partner, or someone whose livelihood depends materially on the organization.

4. **Your office: Founder + President/Executive Director.** I recommend that **Founder** be a historical designation, not an office carrying special constitutional power. Your paid operational title would ultimately be something like **President & Executive Director**, with a functional portfolio covering **Research and Religious Formation**.

   In other words:

   **Founder** = originator of the project, no inherent veto or supremacy.

   **President/Executive Director** = accountable executive employee of the corporation.

   **Research & Religious Formation leadership** = intellectual/religious function, subject to our revelation/doctrine safeguards.

   I would **not** make you Board Chair. The Chair should ultimately be independent.

5. **We need another person to serve as Secretary.** Tennessee requires both a President and Secretary, and those two offices cannot be held by the same individual. Other offices can generally be combined. ([Justia Law][4])

   So an excellent initial arrangement would eventually be:

   **You — Founder, President/Executive Director, Director**

   **Independent Person A — Board Chair, Director**

   **Independent Person B — Secretary, Director**

   Then preferably add separate Treasurer and two additional directors.

   I would avoid making you Treasurer because the entire anti-coercion architecture works better when the founder cannot simultaneously control spiritual direction, executive operations, governance, and money.

6. **Founder compensation: $0 only until an independent approval mechanism exists—not indefinitely.** I do **not** recommend building the organization around the assumption that its founder must work without compensation.

   The rule should instead be:

   **No founder compensation before an independent board approves it.**

   Once the independent directors are seated and the organization has funding, they can approve a reasonable salary prospectively using comparable compensation information and proper documentation. That is precisely the structure the IRS describes for establishing a presumption that insider compensation is reasonable: advance approval by disinterested decision-makers, appropriate comparability data, and contemporaneous documentation. ([IRS][5])

   So I would record your initial compensation status as:

   **“Uncompensated pending independent board approval and financial capacity; reimbursement of legitimate organizational expenses permitted under adopted policy.”**

   Once funding exists, the board should determine what reasonable full-time compensation actually is. We should not arbitrarily suppress it simply because you founded the organization.

7. **Federal status: start as a §501(c)(3) religious public charity, not as an IRS-classified church yet.** This is the most important classification decision.

   Your intended organization may very well **eventually qualify as a church for federal tax purposes**, regardless of whether it uses the English word “church.” The IRS explicitly says the term includes mosques, temples, synagogues and other religious forms. But church status is based on facts and circumstances: congregation, regular services, form of worship, ecclesiastical government, doctrine, ministers, religious literature, etc. ([IRS][6])

   Right now we don't yet have an established congregation.

   The IRS instructions specifically state that an organization without an established congregation may instead be a religious organization that does not yet qualify as a church—and expressly say that it can request church classification **later after establishing a congregation or religious membership group**. ([IRS][6])

   Therefore I recommend initially seeking recognition as a **§501(c)(3) religious public charity**, probably under **§509(a)(1) / §170(b)(1)(A)(vi)** based on public contributions and grants. The IRS describes that classification for organizations substantially supported by governmental grants, public contributions, and other public charities. ([IRS][6])

   Later, when we actually have regular gatherings, a congregation, developed worship, ministers, religious formation and the other relevant facts, we can request reclassification as a church through the IRS determination process. Form 8940 currently provides the mechanism for public-charity reclassification requests. ([IRS][7])

   This approach has another advantage for our anti-coercion project: during the early years, ordinary public-charity reporting gives us a strong transparency discipline rather than relying immediately on the broader filing exceptions churches receive.

8. **Use the full Form 1023.** I recommend that we deliberately prepare the **full Form 1023**, even if some simpler filing route might eventually appear technically available. This organization will have religious activities, research, charitable work, clergy, potentially housing, property, grants, children, and founder compensation. That is exactly the sort of organization for which I want a serious narrative record rather than a minimalist filing.

9. **Fiscal year: December 31.** Locked.

10. **Incorporation approach: prepare first, then file.** I recommend **not filing the Tennessee charter yet**. There are three good reasons: the legal name is still undecided, you currently have only one director, and we do not yet have the second person necessary to occupy the Secretary office.

Technically, Tennessee allows the incorporator to organize the corporation after incorporation and elect the directors then. ([Justia Law][8]) But there is little advantage to rushing the filing when the name and founding board are unresolved.

So our status should be:

**Prepare everything → choose name → recruit founding board → final legal review → file → organizational meeting → EIN → bank/accounting → Form 1023 → public launch.**

**Phase I — Formation:** historical/religious research, publication, website, online religious study, public gatherings, development of worship/practice, fundraising, community formation.

**Phase II — Established religious community:** regular gatherings/worship, pastoral care, formal education, charitable assistance, retreats, clergy/minister formation, regular congregation.

**Phase III — Institutional expansion:** children's ministry, ordination system, paid staff, property acquisition, larger charitable grantmaking.

**Phase IV — High-dependency activities:** intentional community, housing, communal property, residential programs and related institutions.

Housing and intentional community should come relatively late because they are exactly where spiritual, employment, financial and residential power can accidentally become concentrated in one institution.
