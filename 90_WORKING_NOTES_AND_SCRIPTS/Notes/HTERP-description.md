A few findings are already significant.

**First, “revelation” is definitely not one historical phenomenon.** At Mari we have contemporary bureaucratic records describing men and women transmitting messages attributed to named gods; these include temple settings, dreams and apparently ecstatic modes. ([OUP Academic][1]) Shang China gives us something structurally different: physical oracle-bone evidence for a highly organized system by which rulers sought otherwise inaccessible information through ancestors/divine powers. ([British Museum][2])

The Rigveda provides something different again. It is the oldest Sanskrit text, composed in the latter half of the second millennium BCE, and its poets understood sacred language and ritual poetry as penetrating and participating in cosmic order. Its later classification as *śruti*—“heard” revelation—cannot simply be projected backward onto every individual hymn. ([OUP Academic][3])

And **Zarathustra is emerging as exceptionally important**. The Gathas are plausibly very close to him and possibly his own compositions, although authorship, date and homeland retain scholarly uncertainty. Their overwhelming address to Ahura Mazda and their concentration on truth, falsehood and moral choice make Zarathustra one of the strongest non-Israelite ancient candidates for what we might properly call **ethical-prophetic revelation**. ([Iranica Online][4])

Another major result is that **source proximity and metaphysical credibility have to remain separate**. Paul is extraordinarily valuable because his undisputed letters give us first-person claims to visions and revelations: we can establish with unusual confidence that *Paul claimed to have experienced them*. That still doesn't prove what caused them. ([OUP Academic][5]) Conversely, ancient Maya and Shang evidence gives us exceptionally strong proof that divinatory practices actually happened but much poorer access to what the practitioners subjectively experienced.

Muhammad similarly needs to be split into at least two datasets. The **Qur'anic proclamation** is extraordinarily close to the founding period, with early manuscript evidence reinforcing an early Qur'anic corpus; detailed accounts of Muhammad's first revelation belong to a distinct transmitted biographical tradition whose recensions can themselves be reconstructed and compared. ([Cambridge University Press][6]) Treating Qur'an, sīra, hadith and later Islamic theology as one undifferentiated source would therefore be methodologically indefensible.

Perhaps the most consequential methodological result concerns the **controls**.

Controlled psilocybin studies demonstrate that human neurobiology can reproducibly generate experiences displaying unity, sacredness/noetic certainty, transcendence of time and space, positive affect and ineffability—the same family of qualities frequently described as mystical. ([PubMed Central (PMC)][7]) Clinical literature also documents religious/mystical experiences associated with some temporal-lobe neurological phenomena, including sensed divine presence, visions and overwhelming conviction. ([PubMed Central (PMC)][8])

That means:

> **Cross-cultural similarity in mystical experience cannot by itself prove a common transcendent source.**

But the converse inference is equally invalid:

> **Finding a neural mechanism does not prove that transcendent reality does not exist.**

Every ordinary perception—seeing a tree, hearing another human being—is neurologically mediated too.

So the question has become much sharper.

We aren't primarily asking whether people have unusual religious experiences. **They clearly do.**

We're asking whether anything in the corpus **exceeds what we should expect from human neurobiology + culture + information transmission + social processes + fraud**.

That gives us potential evidentiary discriminators such as independently verified unavailable information, genuinely prospective specific predictions, highly specific structures recurring among demonstrably independent cultures, and phenomena surviving adversarial controls.

And another early conclusion is becoming quite strong:

> **The greatest global convergence currently appears at the level of encounter structure, not theological content.**

Across cultures we repeatedly encounter humans attempting to access reality beyond ordinary perception through dreams, voices, visions, divination, sacred speech, possession, contemplation or awakening.

But what supposedly lies beyond ordinary perception differs dramatically:

**one God / many gods / ancestors / spirits / cosmic order / Brahman / Ahura Mazda / YHWH / celestial beings / an impersonal ultimate reality / no creator deity at all.**

That difference is every bit as important as the similarities.

So we have now moved decisively beyond “create a religion combining Judaism, Christianity and Islam.”

The project is now:

# **Humanity's claimed contact with transcendent reality**

and only after that investigation do we ask:

**What, if anything, is actually there?**

**Are apparently different entities culturally interpreted manifestations of something common?**

**Are there multiple noncorporeal intelligences?**

**Is there an ultimate reality beyond those intelligences?**

**Are prophetic encounters categorically different from mystical illumination?**

**Can authentic and inauthentic encounters be distinguished?**

**What survives after institutional theology is stripped away?**

And ultimately:

> **If we rebuilt religion from the surviving evidence rather than inherited religious institutions, what would we actually be justified in believing and practicing?**

That question is now formally downstream of the evidence instead of being assumed at the beginning.
