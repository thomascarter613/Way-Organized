## PRC Phase II-A — Deep Dossiers: Earliest Near Eastern / Israelite Cluster

I have now pushed the corpus past the survey stage. The four most important early cases—**Mari, Balaam, Abraham/Melchizedek, and Moses**—can be reconstructed well enough to expose several mechanisms that will govern the rest of the project.

The principal result is that there is no single clean progression of “Abraham → Moses → Hebrew prophets.” Historically, we are dealing with overlapping traditions about divine communication, older West Semitic divine names, changing conceptions of deity, remembered ancestral figures, and increasingly complex textual redaction.

---

# PRC-001 — Mari Prophetic Corpus

### Historical anchor

This is presently one of our strongest ancient controls.

The archives of Mari on the middle Euphrates preserve eighteenth-century BCE letters from the reigns of Yasmaḫ-Addu and Zimri-Lim. Some letters report prophetic oracles delivered only a day or so before an official wrote to the king. That makes them extraordinarily close to the claimed prophetic event compared with almost everything in the biblical corpus. ([OUP Academic][1])

### What “prophecy” meant there

The basic pattern is recognizable:

**deity → human intermediary → spoken message → official → king**

The prophets were understood as people transmitting messages from particular gods. Messages could warn, encourage, admonish, demand action, or interpret political crises.

Yet Mari prophecy differs significantly from later biblical literary prophecy. Much of it concerns the king, war, dynastic affairs, cultic obligations, territory and divine support. One oracle nevertheless admonishes the king concerning righteousness and justice toward persons who have been wronged. ([OUP Academic][2])

Prophecy at Mari could also be subjected to additional verification through established divinatory procedures. This is significant: prophetic speech was not automatically treated as infallible merely because someone claimed a god had spoken. ([Cambridge Assets][3])

### Entity structure

Mari does **not** support a simple monotheistic model.

Different prophets communicate messages attributed to different deities within an openly polytheistic religious world. Ishtar, Dagan, Adad and others belong to the relevant broader religious environment. Mari's palace religion itself integrated numerous divine figures. ([Cambridge University Press][4])

### Corpus conclusion

Mari establishes something important beyond reasonable historical dispute:

> **Humans claiming to transmit direct messages from divine beings existed in the ancient Near East well before the classical Hebrew prophetic books.**

That means prophecy cannot be explained simply as an invention peculiar to biblical Israel.

But Mari does **not** establish that the entities were real, that all prophets experienced the same phenomenon, or that the gods they named were separate actual intelligences.

It gives us our first high-quality **phenomenological control**.

# PRC-005 — Balaam son of Beor

Balaam is becoming one of the most important cases in the entire research program.

## Independent attestation

The Deir ʿAlla plaster inscription, dating roughly to the late ninth or eighth century BCE, refers to:

**Balaam son of Beor**

and describes him as a **seer of the gods**.

That is striking because the same personal identification appears in the biblical Balaam tradition. ([JSTOR][5])

This is not merely a generic resemblance such as “both cultures had prophets.” We have a **named revelatory personality remembered in more than one religious-literary environment**.

## The Deir ʿAlla encounter

The inscription describes gods coming to Balaam at night, after which he receives or beholds a vision and reports it to an assembly.

The divine world of the inscription is plainly plural. El appears, as do the **šaddayin** and other divine figures. ([JSTOR][6])

The biblical Balaam cycle, meanwhile, makes Balaam ultimately dependent upon YHWH: he can proclaim only what YHWH permits him to say. Oxford's recent overview notes the correspondence between the positive Balaam of Numbers 22–24 and Deir ʿAlla's celebrated “seer of the gods,” while also emphasizing the Bible's later negative portrayals of him. ([OUP Academic][7])

## Why this matters

At least four historical models remain possible.

**Model B1 — historical seer**

There really was a famous Balaam son of Beor whose reputation became preserved by different populations.

**Model B2 — common legendary tradition**

Different communities inherited an older Balaam legend and adapted it.

**Model B3 — Israelite appropriation**

The biblical authors adopted a famous foreign visionary and subordinated his abilities to YHWH.

**Model B4 — reciprocal literary development**

The traditions developed through complicated regional contact and cannot be reduced to simple one-way borrowing.

At present we cannot responsibly select one with certainty.

But the shared name makes coincidence much less satisfactory than it would be for generic similarities.

### Major working inference

Balaam demonstrates a mechanism we need to watch everywhere:

> **One remembered revelatory figure can be incorporated into different theological systems without the underlying figure necessarily originating in either system's final theology.**

That possibility will matter enormously when we reach Jesus, Zarathustra, Muhammad and later founders.

# PRC-002 — Abraham

Abraham requires almost the opposite methodology from Mari.

Mari gives us extremely good contemporary documents but no reason to assume its divine messages are metaphysically true.

Abraham gives us an immensely influential revelatory figure but extremely limited access to the historical individual behind the traditions.

## Historical status

Critical scholarship cannot presently reconstruct a biographical Abraham with confidence. Ronald Hendel's useful framework is that the patriarchal narratives combine cultural memory, folklore, literary construction and potentially genuine old historical memories. ([OUP Academic][8])

Therefore:

**“We cannot historically recover Abraham” ≠ “Abraham never existed.”**

Those are different propositions.

Our status remains:

**Historicity: unresolved.**

## A particularly important divine-name pattern

The patriarchal material repeatedly contains divine terminology based on **El**:

* El
* El Elyon
* El Shaddai
* El Olam
* El Roi
* Elohim

Modern scholarship emphasizes that Hebrew **El** can function as the generic word “god,” but can also correspond to the West Semitic high god El known outside Israel. Compound El names may therefore preserve older manifestations, titles or traditions later associated with YHWH. ([OUP Academic][9])

Hendel further observes that in traditional source-critical reconstruction, the E and P strands portray the patriarchs as knowing God under El/Elohim terminology while the name YHWH becomes disclosed to Moses. ([OUP Academic][10])

That observation deserves much greater weight in this project than we originally gave it.

# PRC-003 — Melchizedek / El Elyon

Melchizedek should no longer be treated merely as an interesting biblical priest.

He may preserve evidence relevant to the transition from broader West Semitic religion into Israelite Yahwism.

## Received Genesis tradition

Genesis presents Melchizedek as a priest associated with:

**El Elyon — “God Most High”**

and connects El Elyon with creation of heaven and earth.

The received form of Genesis ultimately aligns this deity with Abram's own God.

But historically, that equation requires investigation rather than assumption.

## The crucial possibility

One strand of modern scholarship argues that **El Elyon** in Genesis 14 reflects an older West Semitic deity/title and that the explicit reference to **YHWH** in Genesis 14:22 is a later harmonizing gloss identifying El Elyon with YHWH. Robert Cargill develops this argument using comparative Phoenician, Aramaic and related evidence. ([OUP Academic][11])

That is **not a settled scholarly fact**. Cargill advances several revisionist arguments about Genesis 14—including an argument that the Melchizedek narrative itself underwent substantial alteration—which cannot simply be treated as consensus. ([OUP Academic][12])

Our database therefore records:

```text
EL_ELYON_YHWH_IDENTITY

received_text:
    explicit identification / harmonization

historical_origin:
    unresolved

possibility_1:
    El Elyon was always intended as YHWH under a title

possibility_2:
    El Elyon was an older El tradition later identified with YHWH

possibility_3:
    textual/redactional history is more complex than either model
```

## Why Melchizedek matters theologically

The final biblical text itself recognizes a legitimate priest of the Most High God who is:

* outside Israel,
* outside the Mosaic covenant,
* outside the Aaronic priesthood,
* and chronologically before Sinai.

Even if historical reconstruction is impossible, the literary-theological implication is remarkable:

> **The final Genesis tradition does not restrict knowledge or legitimate worship of the supreme God to the later Israelite institutional system.**

That observation will become important when constructing any future theology of revelation outside one covenantal lineage.

# PRC-004 — Moses

Moses sits at the single most consequential transition in this entire early corpus.

## Historical status

There is no straightforward contemporary Egyptian documentation of Moses or the biblical-scale Exodus. Scholarship ranges from fairly skeptical reconstructions to arguments that genuine Egyptian and Exodus memories lie behind the biblical tradition.

Hendel argues that memories of Egyptian oppression, plagues and Moses may reflect historical experiences shaped under Egyptian imperial domination, with Moses possibly representing a dimly remembered historical figure. ([OUP Academic][13])

James Hoffmeier represents a more historically affirmative position and argues for greater authenticity in the Egyptian/Exodus traditions, while acknowledging the extensive scholarly skepticism surrounding Moses and the Exodus. ([OUP Academic][14])

Accordingly:

> **Moses cannot currently receive a high historical-attestation score, but neither should the traditions simply be classified as demonstrated fiction.**

---

# The Exodus 6 anomaly

The text itself gives us one of the strongest clues in the entire divine-name investigation.

Exodus 6 portrays God telling Moses that Abraham, Isaac and Jacob knew the deity as **El Shaddai**, whereas the name **YHWH** was not disclosed to them in the same way.

Yet Genesis elsewhere depicts patriarchs freely using YHWH.

That contradiction is one of the classic pieces of evidence behind Pentateuchal source analysis. ([TheTorah.com][15])

The source-critical explanation is approximately:

```text
tradition A:
YHWH known since primeval / patriarchal times

tradition B:
patriarchs know El / Elohim

tradition C:
patriarchs know El Shaddai
YHWH newly disclosed to Moses

later Torah:
traditions combined
```

The exact old J/E/P reconstruction has been heavily revised in modern scholarship, and source criticism itself has significant critics. But the underlying textual phenomenon—different divine-name traditions being combined—is real and requires explanation. ([OUP Academic][16])

---

# A major historical hypothesis now enters the corpus

There is substantial modern scholarship proposing that **YHWH was originally distinct from the old Canaanite/West-Semitic El tradition**.

Oxford's recent survey notes that scholars have often explained biblical divine names through a process in which originally distinct divine traditions converged. ([OUP Academic][9])

Karel van der Toorn's 2025 history of Israelite religion reconstructs early Iron Age Palestine as religiously diverse, including Canaanite Baal traditions, Aramean/Amorite El traditions and what he characterizes as a desert YHWH tradition, with Israelite religion emerging through their interaction and blending. ([OUP Academic][17])

Daniel Fleming is more cautious about specific southern-origin arguments. He stresses that Egyptian references to **Yhwʒ of the Shasu-land** do not straightforwardly prove the existence of the deity YHWH there. The first certain nonbiblical identification of Yahweh as Israel's god comes much later, in the Iron Age. ([Cambridge University Press][18])

This produces an extremely important distinction.

# Two radically different interpretations of the same evidence

### Historical-development model

```text
Older West Semitic religion
        ↓
El traditions
        +
possibly independent YHWH tradition
        ↓
Israelite interaction / convergence
        ↓
YHWH assimilates El's roles and titles
        ↓
YHWH = El / Elyon / Shaddai / creator
        ↓
exclusive Yahwism
        ↓
mature monotheism
```

This is broadly compatible with much modern historical-critical reconstruction.

### Progressive-identification model

The same data could be interpreted theologically as:

```text
One transcendent reality
        ↓
known partially as El / Elyon / Shaddai
        ↓
patriarchal encounters
        ↓
more specific disclosure to Moses as YHWH
        ↓
progressive clarification of identity
        ↓
eventual recognition that the one encountered
under different names is the sole Creator
```

The historical evidence **does not decide between these models by itself**.

That distinction is now formally encoded into our methodology.

# The entity-identity problem

We now need an explicit identity test.

When two traditions use different divine names, we cannot conclude either:

**different name → different being**

or:

**similar attributes → same being.**

Instead, each identification will be tested on at least eight dimensions:

| Dimension                   | Example question                                                     |
| --------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Ontological rank**        | Is the being supreme, subordinate or one among peers?                |
| **Creation**                | Creator of everything, organizer of preexisting matter, or neither?  |
| **Eternity**                | Uncreated or generated/born?                                         |
| **Moral authority**         | Ultimate moral source or merely powerful?                            |
| **Cosmic sovereignty**      | Universal or geographically/ethnically limited?                      |
| **Relational identity**     | Does the being explicitly identify itself with earlier divine names? |
| **Character continuity**    | Are attributes/actions broadly compatible?                           |
| **Historical transmission** | Is identity likely produced by cultural borrowing or redaction?      |

This is going to be called the:

## **Transcendent Entity Identity Matrix — TEIM**

A divine-name equivalence such as

**El = El Elyon = El Shaddai = YHWH**

will no longer be assumed. It becomes a hypothesis with evidence for and against it.

The same standard will later apply to:

**YHWH ↔ Allah**

**Ahura Mazda ↔ YHWH**

**Brahman/Īśvara ↔ Abrahamic God**

**Ik Onkar ↔ Abrahamic God**

and every other proposed equivalence.

# What the earliest cluster now tells us

We can separate at least **five phenomena** that were previously bundled together as “revelation.”

### A. Immediate prophetic transmission

Mari:

> A particular god communicates a message through a human intermediary.

### B. Cross-cultural seer tradition

Balaam:

> A visionary gains recognition across more than one religious community and is differently theologized by them.

### C. Ancestral encounter tradition

Abraham:

> A remote culture-founder is remembered as encountering a supreme divine being under several El-based titles.

### D. Non-lineage priesthood

Melchizedek:

> A priest outside Israel's later institutional structure worships El Elyon.

### E. Identity-revelation tradition

Moses:

> The divine reality previously associated with the ancestors is identified/disclosed under the specific name YHWH.

Those are fundamentally different structures.

# PRC proposition register — first extraction

I have now begun extracting propositions rather than religions.

### P-0001

> Human beings sometimes report receiving information from apparently nonhuman personal agents.

Supported phenomenologically by: **Mari, Balaam, Moses tradition**, and eventually many later streams.

Metaphysical status: **unresolved**.

### P-0002

> Claimed revelation may occur during ordinary waking experience, dreams, visions or altered states.

Historically strongly supported as a description of reported human experience.

### P-0003

> A prophetic claim may be tested rather than accepted solely on the authority of the claimant.

Strong ancient comparative support from Mari.

### P-0004

> The same revelatory personality can be remembered by communities with different conceptions of the divine world.

Strongly suggested by Balaam.

### P-0005

> A community can reinterpret an inherited divine figure or name within a later theological framework.

Strong comparative plausibility.

### P-0006

> Ancient Israel's own traditions preserve more than one account of when and how the name YHWH became known.

**Very high textual confidence.** ([OUP Academic][10])

### P-0007

> The patriarchal narratives preserve substantial El-based divine terminology.

**Very high textual confidence.** ([OUP Academic][9])

### P-0008

> Whether those El traditions and YHWH originally referred to one deity or became identified historically is a separate question from how the finished Bible understands them.

**Research status: unresolved / competing models.**

### P-0009

> Legitimate access to a high/supreme deity is not restricted, even within the final biblical narrative, to Israelite institutional priesthood.

Supported literarily by Melchizedek and, differently, Balaam.

# Most consequential finding so far

The central question is becoming more precise.

It is no longer merely:

> **“Are different religions talking about the same God?”**

The better question is:

> **“When apparently independent human beings report contact with a transcendent intelligence, what invariant properties—if any—survive after we strip away divine names, languages, mythology, institutions and later theology?”**

That gives us an actual empirical-comparative project.

We can search for invariants such as:

```text
PERSONAL AGENCY
SELF-IDENTIFICATION
TRANSCENDENCE
CREATORSHIP
UNITY
MORAL AUTHORITY
TRUTH DEMAND
JUSTICE DEMAND
COMPASSION
HUMAN ACCOUNTABILITY
REPENTANCE
REVELATION
FREE MORAL CHOICE
POSTMORTEM CONSEQUENCE
UNIVERSALITY
NON-CORPOREALITY
WORSHIP CLAIM
```

Then ask whether those attributes emerge independently or only after known cultural contact.
