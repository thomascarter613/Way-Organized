There are really **two different ways to put HTERP into contact with reality**, and I would begin them in a specific order:

1. **Run the religion on yourself and a very small voluntary group.**
2. **Run the extraordinary claims against prospective tests.**

The first can begin immediately. The second needs considerably more controls.

### Start with the 30-day lived pilot

For the first month, do **not** try to found a formal religion, recruit a congregation, collect tithes, declare clergy, or finalize doctrine. Treat yourself as **Participant 001 in a prototype**.

On Day 0, create a baseline. Write down your present ratings for things such as attention, anger, anxiety, honesty with yourself, generosity, family relationships, sense of meaning, spiritual certainty, sleep, and amount of time spent in prayer/study. Also write short answers to questions like: *What do I currently believe about God? What am I uncertain about? What practices do I actually maintain? What would count as improvement?*

Then follow the existing pilot for thirty days:

* dawn prayer + reading + silence;
* brief midday recollection;
* evening examen;
* one weekly sacred/rest interval;
* one safe fasting or restraint practice;
* regular giving or service;
* the thirty-day Torah → Prophets → Jesus → Qur'an → comparative-witness reading cycle.

Use the journal every day. The important part is recording **before interpretation**. If, for example, you have an unusual dream, sense of presence, synchronicity, apparent answer to prayer, vision, or intuition, write down exactly what happened **before looking for confirmation**.

That gives us a very useful rule:

> **Experience first → timestamped record → interpretation later.**

If you think you received information such as “X will happen,” don't rewrite it afterward. Record precisely:

> September 19, 2026, 7:42 AM — “I had the strong impression that ___ will happen before ___.”

Then leave the text alone. Later we can score it.

That immediately begins producing HTERP-quality data rather than memories.

### Keep three journals instead of one

I would actually split the pilot record into three streams.

**Practice Log** records what you did: prayer, reading, fasting, giving, service, silence, assembly, sleep.

**Experience Log** records unusual experiences without theological interpretation: visual imagery, voice-like cognition, dreams, presence, intuition, emotional states, coincidences.

**Interpretation Log** is written later and says what you think an experience might mean.

For example:

> **Experience:** Woke at 3:12 AM with the vivid impression of someone standing near the bed. Could not move for several seconds.

Then separately:

> **Interpretation A:** spiritual presence.
> **Interpretation B:** sleep paralysis.
> **Confidence:** insufficient evidence.

That tiny discipline is enormously important because it prevents your religious system from manufacturing certainty out of experience.

### At the end of thirty days, test the religion rather than asking whether it “felt spiritual”

Ask whether the system made measurable differences.

Did you become more truthful? More patient? More disciplined? Did you repair more harm? Were you more generous? Did family life improve? Did your sleep deteriorate? Did the framework make you more curious—or more obsessed with signs? Did prayer make you calmer and more ethical, or merely more certain that your intuitions were divine?

Keep practices that reliably produce good fruits without unacceptable costs. Modify neutral ones. Remove harmful ones.

That becomes **Practice Version 0.2**.

Only after that should I recommend involving perhaps **3–8 other adults**, each participating voluntarily and independently.

Crucially, don't tell them what they are “supposed” to experience.

Give them the same practice instructions and journaling method. At Day 30, compare results.

Now you have the beginning of a real community experiment rather than one person's theological imagination.

The second track is much more interesting scientifically.

### Create the HTERP Prospective Registry

This is probably the easiest empirical project to launch.

Whenever anybody claims revelation involving a future event, record:

* exact statement;
* person making it;
* timestamp;
* deadline;
* what exactly counts as success;
* what counts as failure;
* probability if possible;
* what public source will determine the outcome.

Then **cryptographically hash or otherwise immutably timestamp the statement before the outcome**.

Suppose somebody says:

> “God showed me that a magnitude-7+ earthquake will occur in California.”

That's still poor because there is no deadline.

Better:

> “Before March 31, 2027, a USGS-recorded earthquake of magnitude ≥7.0 will occur within California.”

Now it can fail.

Better still:

> “I assign an 80% probability.”

Now it can be compared to ordinary forecasting.

Do that for **every prediction**, including misses.

After 50, 100, or 500 predictions, we can determine whether the claimant is actually outperforming base rates.

That is vastly more informative than collecting impressive fulfilled prophecies retrospectively.

### You can personally test apparent intuitions too

Suppose during prayer you occasionally get strong impressions.

Instead of deciding whether they are from God, create an experimental category:

**PRED-THC-0001**
Timestamp.
Exact impression.
Probability.
Resolution date.

Do this with mundane things too.

After enough trials, you can see whether high-confidence spiritual impressions are actually calibrated.

You might discover:

* they're basically chance;
* they're slightly useful intuition;
* they're extraordinarily accurate;
* accuracy only exists in certain circumstances;
* confidence has no relationship to accuracy.

Every one of those would be an interesting result.

### Hidden-target experiments are even cleaner

We could also conduct simple exploratory experiments without pretending they're publication-quality science.

For instance, have another person use a random process to choose **one image out of 100**.

You don't see which one.

You spend ten minutes in whatever prayer/contemplative state you want and record impressions.

Then compare them with the target.

But the critical part is preventing yourself from saying afterward, “Well, I saw water and technically there was blue in the image.”

Instead, score against **several decoy images**.

You receive five images afterward—one true target and four decoys—without knowing which is correct.

Before reveal, rank all five according to match.

If this happens repeatedly, chance predicts known performance.

That turns:

> “I felt like I received something.”

into:

> “Across 100 blinded trials, I identified the target at rate X versus chance Y.”

Now we're learning something.

### The religious community itself can become a laboratory—but carefully

Eventually a small community could have a **Research Steward** who is deliberately separate from the spiritual teacher.

Whenever somebody says:

> “I think I received a revelation.”

The response would not be:

> “Thus says the Lord.”

Nor:

> “You're hallucinating.”

It would be:

> “Record it.”

Then classify it.

If it concerns someone's personal spiritual life, it may remain private.

If it contains a testable prediction, put it into HTERP-PRED.

If it contains hidden factual information, HTERP-AI.

If it is a new doctrine, it has **zero automatic doctrinal authority**. It enters the evidence/review process.

That could produce a religious culture unlike most historical prophetic movements:

> **revelation is permitted; revelation is not self-authenticating.**

That might become one of the defining ideas of this entire project.

### Do not begin with the hardest experiments

I would sequence the real-world work like this:

1. **You run the 30-day practice pilot.**
2. Start your personal **Experience/PRED Registry** immediately.
3. Afterward, revise the practice to v0.2.
4. Recruit a tiny voluntary pilot group—roughly 3–8 adults.
5. Run the same 30-day pilot independently.
6. Compare results.
7. Start low-cost blinded hidden-target experiments.
8. Establish a public prospective prophecy registry.
9. Only then pursue mediumship/channeling experiments involving outside claimants.
10. If results become scientifically interesting, partner with qualified researchers/statisticians and appropriate ethics review before treating it as formal human-subject research.

There is one thing I would **not** wait on:

**Day 0 can begin now.**

You already have the framework. The next useful artifact would therefore not be another theology document. It would be an **HTERP Pilot Workbook** containing Day 0 baseline instruments, all 30 daily pages, experience-record forms, prophecy-registration forms, weekly reviews, and the Day 30 evaluation.

That would turn what we've built into something you could literally start using tomorrow morning.
