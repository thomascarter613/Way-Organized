What all deliverables are needed to satisfy to IRS that an organization is a "church"

Yes. The important distinction is that there is **no single IRS document that turns an organization into a “church.”** For federal tax purposes, the IRS evaluates the organization under a **facts-and-circumstances test**. It expressly says that a church does **not** have to satisfy every traditional characteristic, although a **congregation or other religious membership group that regularly meets for worship is generally required**. ([IRS][1])

There are really **three separate things to prove**:

**1. The entity qualifies under §501(c)(3).**
**2. Its actual operations qualify it specifically as a “church” under §§509(a)(1) and 170(b)(1)(A)(i).**
**3. If you want an IRS determination letter explicitly recognizing that status, you submit the full Form 1023 and Schedule A.**

Churches that actually meet §501(c)(3) are generally tax-exempt **without filing Form 1023**, and their donors may claim deductions even without a determination letter. But an organization seeking formal IRS recognition **as a church cannot use Form 1023-EZ**; the IRS says it should file the full Form 1023. ([IRS][2])

### The IRS's 14 church characteristics

The matrix in item 53 should explicitly address the IRS's recognized characteristics: distinct legal existence; creed and form of worship; ecclesiastical government; doctrine and discipline; religious history; membership; ministers; substantive ministerial training; religious literature; established worship location; regular congregation; regular services; youth religious instruction; and ministerial preparation. The IRS does **not** require all fourteen, and these characteristics are evaluated together with the organization's actual facts and circumstances. ([IRS][1])

There is one factor I would treat as qualitatively more important than most of the others: **an actual congregation**. Current IRS Form 1023 instructions say that although all characteristics need not be present, an organization claiming church classification is **generally required to have a congregation or other religious membership group**. Schedule A asks whether people regularly attend and participate at an established location, how membership works, member rights and benefits, average service attendance, and whether members are related to each other. ([IRS][1])

That means a beautiful charter, creed, canon law, ordination manual and website **by themselves would not be enough**. There should be a real religious community regularly doing what the documents describe.

### What I would consider the “minimum mature filing state”

I would not rush the IRS application immediately after incorporation. For an organization starting from scratch, the strongest application would show some **history of actual operations**: a constituted governing body, adopted creed/doctrine, bona fide members, regular worship, recurring attendance, active ministers, worship records, financial records and actual religious activity.

The IRS's Schedule A gets remarkably concrete. It asks about the written creed, literature, doctrine and discipline, ecclesiastical hierarchy, form of worship, **days and times of services**, the **order of events in worship**, **average attendance**, the worship location, congregation and membership process, religious rites, youth religious instruction and ministerial training. ([IRS][1])

An established place does **not** have to be a church-owned building. The IRS explicitly says the organization may **own it, rent it, or be permitted to use it free of charge**. ([IRS][1])

Likewise, ministerial preparation doesn't necessarily require an accredited seminary. The IRS says the prescribed course of study may be **formal or informal**, but merely self-ordination or purchasing an ordination certificate without substantive study does not count. ([IRS][5])

### The actual filing

If we want the IRS to issue a determination letter stating that the organization is recognized as a **church/public charity under §509(a)(1) and §170(b)(1)(A)(i)**, the filing would essentially be:

**Form 1023 + Schedule A + the organizing documents + financial information + activity narrative + supporting exhibits + user fee.**

And we would select the public-charity classification corresponding to **§509(a)(1) / §170(b)(1)(A)(i)**. ([IRS][1])

Once recognized as a church, an organization generally does **not** file Form 990, 990-EZ or 990-N merely because it is tax exempt. Other returns can still apply—for example employment-tax returns or Form 990-T for sufficient unrelated business income. ([IRS][6])

One more important point: **“religious organization” and “church” are not synonymous for IRS purposes.** A religious nonprofit can qualify under §501(c)(3) without qualifying for the special federal classification of a church. The latter requires the additional institutional/congregational facts we've just outlined. ([IRS][7])

For the organization we've been developing, I would turn these **54 deliverables into a formal document architecture**—something like **CH-001 through CH-054**—and map every document directly to **Form 1023, Schedule A, the 14 church characteristics, §501(c)(3), §509(a)(1), and §170(b)(1)(A)(i)**. That would give us a concrete completion checklist from incorporation all the way through a submission-ready IRS church-recognition binder.

Do congregants have to meet in person

---

Not necessarily in the sense that **every service must be in person**, but if the goal is specifically to obtain and defend **IRS classification as a “church,” an entirely online congregation is materially riskier** under current federal guidance and case law.

The IRS says that a church is generally expected to have “a congregation or other religious membership group that meets regularly for religious worship.” Its current Form 1023 instructions define an “established congregation” as people who regularly attend and participate in religious services **at an established location**, and define an established place of worship as a place where regularly scheduled services are held; that place may be owned, rented, or provided free. ([IRS][1])

The strongest judicial authority I found cuts against relying on a purely virtual congregation. In *Foundation of Human Understanding v. United States*, the Federal Circuit considered an organization that argued its radio and Internet listeners formed a “virtual congregation.” The court rejected that theory because simply receiving religious teachings electronically did not establish the associational component of communal worship. What mattered was whether congregants had a meaningful opportunity to **associate with one another in worship**. ([Federal Circuit Court][2])

That case is especially important because the same organization had previously been recognized as a church when it was conducting regular services for physical congregations of roughly 50–350 people. After its in-person congregational activity diminished and its ministry became predominantly broadcast/Internet-based, it could no longer establish church status. ([Federal Circuit Court][2])

So I would distinguish four models:

| Model                                                                       | IRS church-classification risk             |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Regular congregation physically meets weekly                                | **Strongest**                              |
| Regular in-person congregation + livestream/Zoom participation              | **Strong**                                 |
| Mostly online, but congregation periodically gathers physically for worship | **Potentially defensible, fact-dependent** |
| 100% online congregation with no physical gatherings                        | **Significant classification risk**        |

The key point is that **online ministry itself is perfectly compatible with §501(c)(3)**. An organization can be a tax-exempt religious organization while operating largely or entirely online. The question is narrower: whether it qualifies for the special federal classification of a **“church” under §§509(a)(1) and 170(b)(1)(A)(i)**. Federal law distinguishes a church from the broader category of religious organizations. ([Department of Justice][3])

There is also an important nuance for a modern interactive service. The Federal Circuit case involved broadcasts/Internet transmissions that lacked genuine congregant-to-congregant communal interaction. A live Zoom service where members pray together, speak with one another, participate in rites, receive pastoral ministry, and form an ongoing community is factually different. But I would **not rely on that distinction as the sole basis of an IRS church application**, because the current Form 1023 instructions still describe the congregation as regularly participating “at an established location.” ([IRS][1])

For the organization we're designing, therefore, I would use a **hybrid model**. It can be digital-first and have members anywhere, but establish at least one recurring physical congregational gathering. It need not initially own a church building. The IRS expressly accepts a worship location that is rented or provided free of charge. ([IRS][4])

For example, we could structure it so that there is a principal congregation meeting every Sabbath/Sunday/other designated worship day at a rented room, residence, community facility, or other established location, while simultaneously livestreaming the service and operating a much larger online community. Additional geographically dispersed groups could later become local congregations.

That would let us honestly answer the crucial Schedule A questions along these lines: **yes**, regularly scheduled religious services; **yes**, established congregation; **yes**, established place of worship; then provide the service schedule, average attendance, location arrangement, membership process, worship order, and supporting records. ([IRS][5])

One thing I would **not** do is fabricate an in-person congregation merely to satisfy the IRS. The congregation and worship activity need to be substantively real. An older IRS analysis specifically warns that nominal documentation does not substitute for an actual body of worshippers regularly assembling together. ([IRS][6])

So, practically: **no, everyone does not have to attend in person, and the church can have a very large online component. But I would establish a genuine recurring physical congregation if we intend to seek formal IRS recognition specifically as a church.**

If you want, the next useful step is to design the **minimum viable congregation**: how many people we realistically need, how often they should meet, where they can meet, what a qualifying worship service should contain, what attendance/membership records to keep, and how long I would establish that operating history before filing Form 1023.
