I think the naming problem is actually telling us something important about what the institution is.

**The community** — the people participating in it.
**The institution** — the legal and administrative organization that supports the community.

Both are useful precisely because their original meanings are broader than the English religious buildings they later became associated with.

**Ekklesia / ecclesia** (Greek **ἐκκλησία, ekklēsía**) means an **assembly, gathering, or congregation of people**. In classical Greek it could refer to a civic assembly. Early Christians adopted the word for the gathered community of believers. So when the New Testament is translated as “church,” *ekklesia* usually does **not inherently mean a church building**. It means something closer to “the assembly” or “the community gathered together.”

That distinction could be valuable for your project. **Ecclesia** can imply *the gathered people* rather than a denomination, hierarchy, or sacred building.

**Masjid** (Arabic **مسجد, masjid**) literally means **“place of prostration”** or **“place where one bows/prostrates.”** It comes from the Arabic root **s-j-d (س ج د)**, associated with prostrating oneself, especially before God. English **mosque** ultimately derives from *masjid* through European forms of the word.

So conceptually:

| Term                    | Literal/basic sense                                       | Emphasis                                   |
| ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| **Ekklesia / Ecclesia** | assembly, gathering                                       | **the people gathered**                    |
| **Masjid**              | place of prostration                                      | **the place/action of worship**            |
| **Synagogue**           | gathering/assembly                                        | **coming together**                        |
| **Church**              | historically “Lord's [house]” in the Germanic word-family | eventually both **community and building** |

There is an interesting convergence here. **Ekklesia** and **synagogue** both fundamentally describe **gathering**, while **masjid** describes what humans do in relation to the divine—**prostrate themselves**.

For what you are designing, that suggests we may want an institutional word whose primitive meaning is something like:

**“the gathering of human beings seeking/encountering the Transcendent.”**

Rather than a word meaning “the building belonging to our particular religion.”

That opens a very interesting naming direction: perhaps we shouldn't invent another equivalent of *church/mosque/synagogue* at all. We could develop a term for **the assembly**, another for **the sacred gathering/place**, and another for **the tradition itself**—much as older religious languages naturally distinguished these concepts.

The comparison actually strengthens the case for **not simply choosing one inherited religious term** as our umbrella word.

Historically, several traditions developed remarkably similar concepts for “the gathered community”:

* Greek **ekklesia (ἐκκλησία)** = assembly/congregation; it could be an ordinary civic assembly before becoming strongly associated with Christianity. In the Septuagint it frequently translates Hebrew *qahal*. ([Bible Hub][1])
* Hebrew **qahal (קהל)** = assembly, congregation, convocation—the people gathered as an organized body. ([Bible Hub][2])
* Greek **synagōgē (συναγωγή)** literally carries the idea of “bringing together” or an assembly, before *synagogue* became specifically associated with Jewish communal worship. ([Etymology Online][3])
* Sanskrit/Pali **saṅgha** means community, association, or assembly, though religious usage now carries specifically Buddhist associations. ([Etymology Online][4])
* Arabic **masjid** takes a different route: instead of naming the community, it names the **place/action of prostration**.

So there are really several different things hiding inside our English word *church*.

### I think we should build our own vocabulary

I would define at least **six separate concepts**:

| Concept                            | What it means                                                          | Proposed term                      |
| ---------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| **Tradition**                      | The developing religious/spiritual way as a whole                      | **The Way** / eventual proper name |
| **Universal community**            | Everyone participating in the tradition                                | **The Confluence**                 |
| **Local community**                | A geographically or digitally organized community                      | **a Confluence**                   |
| **Gathered meeting**               | People actually assembling for worship, inquiry, teaching, meals, etc. | **an Assembly**                    |
| **Sacred/place of gathering**      | The physical space used for practice                                   | **House of Encounter**             |
| **Research/intellectual function** | Historical study of transcendent encounter                             | **HTERP**                          |

That creates a coherent language without claiming that we're another species of church, synagogue, mosque, or Buddhist sangha.

Someone could say:

> “I belong to the Confluence.”

> “Our local Confluence gathers on Sunday.”

> “We're holding an Assembly tonight.”

> “The Assembly is being held at the House of Encounter.”

These sound surprisingly natural.

## Why I wouldn't call the whole thing *Ecclesia*

**Ecclesia** is attractive because its original meaning fits extraordinarily well. It means essentially **the assembly**.

But after roughly two millennia of Christianity, *ecclesia*, *ecclesial*, and *ecclesiology* are overwhelmingly perceived as Christian vocabulary. Calling ourselves **The Ecclesia** would therefore subtly tell outsiders that this is some novel Christian denomination.

Similarly:

**Qahal** → reads Jewish/Hebrew.

**Masjid** → reads Muslim.

**Sangha** → reads Buddhist.

**Ashram** → reads Hindu.

**Temple** → carries several traditions but suggests the building rather than the people.

I don't think any one civilization's inherited word should be privileged as the universal term if one of our foundational principles is that the phenomenon we're investigating is **prior to the later institutional traditions that interpret it**.

That principle is important.

We are essentially proposing:

**Encounter → testimony → interpretation → tradition → institution**

rather than beginning with:

**institution → doctrine → interpretation of everything else.**

That suggests the vocabulary should start with **Encounter**, not with Christianity, Islam, Judaism, Buddhism, etc.

## This gives us two exceptionally strong words

### **Encounter**

This may be the most important theological word in the entire project.

It refers to the claimed human encounter with whatever ultimately exists—God, gods, the Absolute, Brahman, Dao, transcendent intelligence, ultimate reality, mystical unity, revelation, illumination, or something for which our existing categories prove inadequate.

Importantly, **Encounter doesn't prejudge what was encountered.**

That is exactly what our historical phenomenology needs.

### **Confluence**

This describes what happens **afterward**.

Many streams of human testimony arrive from different cultures and eras.

They meet.

They may agree.

They may contradict one another.

They may partially overlap.

One might preserve something another lost.

And none needs to be prematurely erased.

So I can see a very elegant two-word conceptual architecture emerging:

> **Encounter is the phenomenon.**
>
> **Confluence is the community.**

That is much stronger than merely finding a clever synonym for *church*.

# A possible complete religious vocabulary

I'd provisionally use:

**The Transcendent**
Our deliberately broad term for the ultimate reality toward which religious encounter points, without prematurely defining its ontology.

**Encounter**
An alleged or experienced contact with transcendent reality.

**Witness**
Someone reporting or preserving testimony concerning Encounter.

**Testimony**
The human account of an Encounter.

**Tradition**
A historical stream through which testimony, interpretation and practice have been transmitted.

**Confluence**
A community in which those streams can meet without requiring that they first become identical.

**Assembly**
A particular gathering of the Confluence.

**Practice**
Disciplines intended to cultivate ethical, contemplative, communal or transcendent life.

**Inquiry**
The disciplined investigation of claims concerning transcendent reality.

**Discernment**
The process by which an individual or community evaluates experiences, testimony and interpretations.

**House of Encounter**
A physical place maintained for Assembly, contemplation, study, hospitality and practice.

That already begins to feel like an actual religious civilization rather than a repackaged denomination.

## And there's a deeper theological implication

We may eventually be able to say:

> **No religion owns the Encounter.**

Judaism preserves certain testimony about it.

Christianity preserves certain testimony about it.

Islam preserves certain testimony about it.

Hindu, Buddhist, Jain, Sikh, Daoist, indigenous, shamanic, mystical and philosophical traditions preserve other testimony.

The Confluence becomes the place where humanity's accumulated testimony is **preserved, practiced, compared, criticized and discerned** rather than prematurely harmonized.

That is considerably more interesting than conventional “interfaith religion.”

# **The Confluence**

**The Confluence** — community
